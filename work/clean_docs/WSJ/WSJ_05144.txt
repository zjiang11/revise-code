A Biden presidency will change much about U.S. foreign policy, from repairing America's alliances and rejoining international agreements to ending infatuations with dictators. But it is worth considering where President Trump leaves a legacy on which his successor can build.
Mr. Biden, if he plans to help repair the rifts in this nation, may want to say a few nice things about his predecessor. More to the point, if the Biden team rejects all of the Trump administration's foreign-policy thought categorically, it will miss opportunities. In some cases, Mr. Trump hired people like Jim Mattis who accomplished good things and set the stage for more. In other cases, even where Mr. Trump ultimately flailed or failed -- as with his efforts to denuclearize North Korea, or create a better nuclear deal with Iran -- his administration has sometimes shifted the debate in ways that could help his successor. Consider:
-- The U.S. defense buildup and its focus on Russia and China. The defense budget is now about $740 billion. President Obama's budgets in the $600 billion range were well above Cold War averages. But given the strains on the armed forces from two decades at war in the Middle East, and frequent budgetary delays due to a dysfunctional Washington, these levels were not enough to ensure readiness while also focusing on the future.
The Obama administration's Pentagon team had good ideas for modernizing the military, as well as a program to put more U.S. and other North Atlantic Treaty Organization forces in Poland and the Baltic states to deter Russia. But many of those changes began around 2014 and were only partly implemented before the Obama-Biden administration ended.
Under Defense Secretary Mattis, and then Mark Esper, readiness levels have improved significantly -- for example, a typical aircraft squadron or wing might now have 80% of its planes fully operational at a given time, rather than 60%. More investments are being made in stealthy long-range aircraft, unmanned systems in the air and on or under the seas, hypersonic missiles, cybersecurity, small satellites and artificial intelligence.
As important, Moscow and Beijing have heard loud and clear that the U.S. is freshly determined to challenge their aggressive ways. If anything, the Pentagon now verges on overdoing it, and Mr. Biden should look for ways to ratchet back tensions -- even as the Defense Department continues to invest in new technologies and ideas.
-- Negotiating with Pyongyang. Mr. Trump flip-flopped badly on North Korea. He went from threatening war in 2017, after North Korea's missile and nuclear tests, to cozying up to Kim Jong Un, to having no realistic road map for negotiating. Expecting North Korea to eliminate its nuclear arsenal at once repeated the errors of previous administrations. But direct contact with Mr. Kim is no longer such a radical idea. Mr. Biden can use the channels of communication Mr. Trump opened up to propose a more realistic deal -- dismantling North Korea's ability to make any more bombs in exchange for a partial lifting of sanctions. Such an interim deal might have been politically unthinkable before Mr. Trump broke down barriers to dialogue and compromise with the North.
-- Improving trade accords. Ironically, after trashing the Obama administration's proposed Trans-Pacific Partnership, Mr. Trump wound up using many of its ideas and higher standards on intellectual property, environmental protection and labor rights to improve Nafta. The U.S.-Mexico-Canada trade pact will likely go down as one of his greater foreign-policy accomplishments. Perhaps now Mr. Biden can swing the ideas of the USMCA back to the Pacific and create a broader concept for TPP that will be more politically palatable in the U.S.
-- Reducing the risk of war with Russia. Mr. Trump was far too affectionate and forgiving toward Vladimir Putin; fortunately, bipartisan resolve in Congress prevented his administration from going soft on the Russian strongman, and sanctions were strengthened. But Mr. Trump wasn't wrong to sense that the current heightened state of U.S.-Russia tensions is unnecessary and dangerous.
A big part of the problem has been Russia's predictable reaction to the Bush administration's promises of further NATO expansion. Aiming instead for a new accord with Russia that replaces NATO's vision of membership for Ukraine and Georgia with a more realistic framework for neutrality in this part of Eastern Europe -- in exchange for Russia's agreeing to resolve its territorial disputes with these countries -- would turn Mr. Trump's instinct into a productive approach. A deal wouldn't transform Mr. Putin into a nice guy, but it could reduce the risks of war.
-- Improving the Iran nuclear deal. Mr. Trump's demands on Tehran amount to an insistence on regime change. That is excessive and unrealistic. But he has created a new normal in the relationship that gives the next administration an opportunity. Rather than return the U.S. to the 2015 nuclear deal next year -- only two years before its terms were set to relax -- Mr. Biden should seek a modified deal that extends indefinitely the restrictions on Iran's nuclear activities in exchange for lifting most sanctions.
Mr. Trump leaves his successor a dangerous world and an unsteady U.S. foreign policy. But a Biden-Harris administration should look to exploit those situations where the Trump administration has either set down worthwhile building blocks for future policy or disrupted pre-existing paradigms enough that good new things may now be possible.
---
Mr. O'Hanlon is a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution.

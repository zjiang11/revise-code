In the Associated Press newsroom a decade ago, I witnessed the birth of a new era: AI penning news. Before artificial intelligence rose to its current prominence, it wasn't tech luminaries but journalists at AP who floated the audacious idea of machines taking up editorial roles. As I watched lines of code spin stories, a thought nagged at me: Weren't stories meant to be earned, not generated?
Fast-forward to today, and this once-controversial shift has proved revolutionary for AP and many other organizations. Tackling two monumental challenges in journalism -- covering an ever-expanding breadth of news and overcoming the limits of human capacity -- AI has reshaped the industry. AI enabled AP to broaden its quarterly financial reporting from 400 companies to 4,000. This had ripple effects: Stanford researchers found that such comprehensive coverage spurred trading activity and heightened liquidity for the newly covered firms, bolstering the market's overall vitality.
Today's emergence of advanced large language models promises even greater transformation. Beyond reporting, they assist in deep-diving into big data, quantifying the occurrence of events, and revealing insights that were once obscured. With checks for biases and paired with human discernment, AI tools can condense mountains of documents into precise narratives at breathtaking speed. This melding of human intuition and machine precision puts journalism on the brink of becoming a faster yet more analytical profession.
Journalism is valuable not for what it summarizes but for what it reveals. It often relies on firsthand knowledge gained through conversations with human sources, sometimes involving dozens, if not hundreds, of interviews. AI bots take in every news article published, every interview carried out and every story honed, with little restraint. As these algorithms comb through the digital universe, the diligent work of journalists and many other authors becomes their primary textbooks.
The implicit contract here is clear: For AI to flourish, journalism must thrive. Neglecting the health of journalism doesn't only impair its own future but risks feeding AI with inferior data. That could set the stage for the dreaded "model collapse." Thus, in AI's evolution, the products of human journalism are golden. As tech giants capitalize on mined data, the source of this information -- the news industry -- often remains unacknowledged and unrewarded. It's time we settled this debt.
We must acknowledge the disparities. Many news entities, repositories of invaluable data, grapple with cash limitations. AI firms with ample financial resources face a dearth of high-caliber data. Were the economist David Ricardo to observe this modern conundrum, he'd likely draw parallels with his theory of comparative advantage and suggest a market-driven remedy: a collaboration in which both sectors take advantage of their respective strengths.
Determining a fair price for news data used in AI training is a complex task. The New York Times's copyright-infringement lawsuit against AI companies marks a significant shift. It could become a landmark case, setting a precedent for how the legal system values news content used in training large language models and determining payment for past use.
Publishers and AI companies need clear guidelines as their relationship evolves. While major news organizations may have the upper hand in negotiating compensation, smaller players risk being overshadowed, which could lead to disparities within the industry.
As prominent media outlets seek payment from AI developers for content use, it's crucial to find approaches that benefit all parties involved. Revenue-sharing models akin to Shutterstock's Contributor Fund, which compensates content creators for using their work in AI training, could promote more equitable benefit sharing. This requires understanding how the data are used.
Publisher-AI relationships are evolving amid heightened demand for transparency in algorithmic data use and increasing regulatory scrutiny. As the industry champions these causes, we must ensure that innovation isn't hamstrung. The advent of the telegraph in 1846 led five New York daily newspapers to pool their resources, leading to the creation of the Associated Press. The AI era offers similar collaborative potential. By joining forces, news entities can devise systems and incentives that allow AI companies, especially startups and independent developers, access to quality training data, fostering a mutually beneficial environment.
The crux is acknowledging journalism's invaluable role in AI's ascent. While this contribution remains significant, the essence of journalistic principles is being challenged. With tech giants stepping into shoes traditionally worn by veteran editors, the rich tapestry of journalism faces potential dilution.
We're already seeing AI-generated summaries from search engines, offering news snippets from various online sources. Such automated digests carry the risk of overshadowing the myriad journalistic perspectives that underpin our global discourse. Instead of broadening horizons by exposing readers to a spectrum of viewpoints, these summaries could unwittingly constrain understanding. With tech companies as both AI upstream developers and downstream distributors, their responsibility intensifies. It's imperative that they recognize this and work with news organizations, striving for authenticity and a balanced representation of facts.
The line between human and machine-written content has been blurred, and the onus doesn't lie only with the producers of information. News consumers have to become adept at discerning what they're encountering: facts, opinions or algorithms. If AI plays a role in shaping or propagating a narrative, its involvement should be made clear. Differentiating between varied sources of information becomes the cornerstone of journalism's integrity and trustworthiness.
AI tools are poised to become mainstays in newsrooms globally. The path may be charged, but the essence of story telling must endure. Beyond being mere data points for training algorithms, journalism acts as the moral compass, guiding the transparency and ethics inherent in AI. It's time to address and redress that contribution to the AI realm.
---
Mr. Marconi is CEO of AppliedXL, a New York based company specializing in AI and computational journalism. He has worked as the Journal's research-and-development chief and as the Associated Press's AI co-lead.

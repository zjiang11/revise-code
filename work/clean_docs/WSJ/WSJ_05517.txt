Rebecca Boyle
"Could I have been a parking lot attendant?" Dave Matthews sings. "Could I have been anyone other than me?" Two of my favorite novels of 2024 pose similar questions. In Kaliane Bradley's "The Ministry of Time," the British government can time-travel, importing a handful of people from various eras of the empire's past. The book follows a young woman of British-Cambodian descent, assigned to befriend an "expat" time traveler, the naval officer Graham Gore, who died on John Franklin's doomed Arctic expedition in the 1840s. The Ministry snatches people just before their demise, reasoning that if they were about to die anyway, their transport to the future won't create temporal problems. Gore and others, including a gay World War I soldier, an Elizabethan woman and a Jamestown settler, join the 21st century with varying degrees of success. Gore is horrified that the empire is no more and that he and his female "bridge" will cohabit; his displacement in time becomes a metaphor for the experience of immigrants, traditionalists and explorers alike. Though it's mostly a sexy sci-fi thriller, "The Ministry of Time" grapples with questions like how to adapt to a world whose climate, both physical and political, has changed before one's eyes and how traumatic events like Auschwitz and 9/11 form our futures. Questions of choice also animate "The Husbands," by Holly Gramazio, in which a single woman walks home from a night at a bar with friends to find a man in her home. He insists that he is her husband and shows photos of their wedding to prove it. When he enters their attic to retrieve something, a new husband -- and a new life -- emerge. Alternate husbands and lives arrive in quick succession as the narrator grapples with the work of love, the responsibility of choice and both the magic and drudgery of building a life. Both books left me considering unexpected consequences, but if we are allowed to make choices -- and we make them out of love -- the future is ours to create.
-- Ms. Boyle is the author of "Our Moon: How Earth's Celestial Companion Transformed the Planet, Guided Evolution, and Made Us Who We Are."

Yuval Levin
Technologies are tools of convenience, but when we let them mediate between us and the world we give them the power to transform us. Christine Rosen, in "The Extinction of Experience: Being Human in a Disembodied World," draws on a deep reservoir of knowledge with unfaltering clarity and accessibility to show us how we have let our tools rob us of the kinds of human experiences that ought to form our character, and in the process have been giving ourselves terrible habits that leave us miserable. The book brims with humor and style, but don't let that fool you: It's meant to terrify you into taking better care of your soul, and it succeeds.
-- Mr. Levin is the author, most recently, of "American Covenant: How the Constitution Unified Our Nation -- and Could Again."
Daniel J. Levitin
Sometimes it feels that life is a burned-out cigarette. In these dark moments, we all could use a light. Enter Scarlet Keys, a successful and talented songwriter and a professor in the esteemed songwriting program at Berklee College of Music, hit by a surprise diagnosis of cancer. She recovered -- remarkably, admirably and poignantly. "What if It All Goes Right? Practicing Hope in the Hardest Times" is her guide to herself and offers a lasting companion for anyone facing challenges, from small daily ups and downs to the most relationship- and life-threatening urgencies. Ms. Keys's poetic writing style brings together quotes that are at once uplifting and grounding, combining them with her own routines and checklists for staying hopeful. As the philosopher John Gray has said, hope is not unbridled optimism; it is a state of being that can evoke the very best in us. This is not a self-help book but a self-care book that I find myself referring to daily.
-- Mr. Levitin is a musician and neuroscientist. His most recent book is "I Heard There Was a Secret Chord: Music as Medicine."
Sarah London
Reading is my happy place. I'll read anything good, and I am always reading something. There's no place for book snobbery in my library, which contains many romance novels. (The truth will set you free -- when I shared this with a table of other senior healthcare executives, they all admitted they also were racing through "Fourth Wing" by Rebecca Yarros.) Other favorites I read this year include "The Ministry of Time" by Kaliane Bradley, "The Investigator" by John Sandford and "The Outsiders" by William Thorndike. I also caught up with Inspector Gamache in Louise Penny's "A World of Curiosities." He's the fictional character I quote the most when I talk about leadership, especially his line about the four sentences that lead to wisdom: I'm sorry. I was wrong. I need help. I don't know.
-- Ms. London is the CEO of Centene.
Shirley MacLaine
This year, I'm reading more about the universe, and what is really going on, and how we should open up to it. How we are blinded to true reality. I recommend "UFO . . . Contact From the Pleiades," volumes I and II, by Brit and Lee Elders, now out in a 45th-anniversary edition. I only read books written by legitimate people who had the actual experience. My reading habits have changed -- so much written about the human mind, about right and wrong, and I would rather hear about what we're not admitting. How we're not alone. Get this book, read the book!

Kathryn McLay
This year I came across Lisa-Jo Baker's memoir, "It Wasn't Roaring, It Was Weeping." Ms. Baker spent most of her childhood in South Africa before the end of apartheid, with a couple of formative periods in the United States, before moving here permanently around the time of Nelson Mandela's historic election. The book explores her family's past as white South Africans, in the context of social and political changes there, including her father's anger, the shared trauma of witnessing blatant and inhumane discrimination, and the desire for equality. Understanding these contradictions is at the heart of a story she tells with heartbreaking honesty. I was also captivated by "Young China: How the Restless Generation Will Change Their Country and the World" by Zak Dychtwald and was privileged recently to meet the author. His book provides a better understanding of the cultural subtext of Generation Z growing up in China. Like the rest of the reading public, it seems, I loved "Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow" by Gabrielle Zevin. Her characters remain with me.
-- Ms. McLay is the president and CEO of Walmart International.
H.R. McMaster
I took a break from foreign policy and national security to read five books relevant to regaining confidence in our democratic principles, institutions and processes. In "This Is Not Who We Are: America's Struggle Between Vengeance and Virtue," Zachary Shore reminds us that contending conceptions of national character are not new. His telling of how Americans' disposition toward mercy overcame the impulse for revenge after World War II is inspiring. Three new books offer insights regarding the orthodoxy of self-loathing that animated the pro-Hamas, anti-Israel, antisemitic and anti-American protests at U.S. universities after the Oct. 7, 2023, mass murder, hostage-taking, rape and infanticide attacks on Israel. In "Morning After the Revolution: Dispatches From the Wrong Side of History," Nellie Bowles writes with wit and insight about how a liberal intelligentsia has shifted far-left interpretations of history and contemporary affairs into the mainstream. In "On Settler Colonialism: Ideology, Violence, and Justice," Adam Kirsch succinctly describes how some academics have promoted an irrational -- and internally inconsistent -- concept that warps the way many younger people think about world history. In "The End of Race Politics: Arguments for a Colorblind America," Coleman Hughes dismantles the specious arguments that underpin postmodernist critical theories and demonstrates how they promote a new form of racism that undermines confidence in our common humanity and American identity. Finally, Wright Thompson, in his magisterial "The Barn: The Secret History of a Murder in Mississippi," reminds us that we Americans have often failed to live up to our principles, showing us how to take in the ugliest landscapes of our history, nurture our republic and make progress toward keeping those principles. These are important books because, as the philosopher Richard Rorty observed: "National pride is to countries what self-respect is to individuals: a necessary condition for self-improvement."
-- Retired Lt. Gen. McMaster is the author, most recently, of "At War With Ourselves: My Tour of Duty in the Trump White House."
Danielle Pletka
As a second Trump administration approaches, there are pressing questions about the president's plans for Ukraine. He has promised a "24 hour" resolution to the war, but his strategy remains opaque. Many fear it will mean abandoning Kyiv to Vladimir Putin. But if you read Yaroslav Trofimov's masterly "Our Enemies Will Vanish: The Russian Invasion and Ukraine's War of Independence," you will understand that much of the war was lost in that fateful first year of 2022. Three of Mr. Trofimov's points resonate deeply: First, this Ukraine is not the Ukraine of either the Nazi or the Soviet years. It is no longer a nation of blood and soil but a nation, like America, based on an idea of freedom. Second, had Joe Biden been less obsessed with escalation and more with Ukrainian victory over our shared enemy, the war may well have been won. Last, when we look back and ask how we began the slow march to the next great war, it will be hard to ignore America and Europe's feckless lack of resolve in supporting our democratic ally.
-- Ms. Pletka is a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute.
Devika Rege
One of my favorite reads in 2024 was Colm Toibin's "On James Baldwin." This year marks a century since the American icon's birth, though Baldwin appears on social-media feeds these days more for his prescient solidarity with the people of Palestine. It is fitting, then, that Mr. Toibin's quietly daring tribute challenges his own imaginative capacity to comprehend his literary hero across multiple axes of intersectionality. The result is both a profound meditation on craft and a deeply moving experiment in what a poetics of solidarity might entail. Closer to home, 2024 also marks a decade of India under Narendra Modi, and anthropologist Alpa Shah's "The Incarcerations" recounts the Kafkaesque ordeal and backstories of the BK-16: a group of human rights defenders who were imprisoned without trial for allegedly conspiring against the government. A finalist for the Orwell Prize, this blistering tour de force lays bare the plight of India's minorities and how ruthlessly dissent has been suppressed. Yet I found myself inspired. "The battle for the soul of democracy is not over yet," Ms. Shah reminds us. "This book, its readership and dissemination, is part of its protection."
-- Ms. Rege is the author of "Quarterlife," a novel.
Andrew Roberts
My choices are very much of the moment and ought to be read by people who are concerned with current affairs. Matt Pottinger's "The Boiling Moat" is about what the West must do urgently to deter Chinese President Xi Jinping from invading Taiwan. The saber-rattling and air and sea incursions are ramping up, and policymakers need to put all of Mr. Pottinger's suggestions into operation quickly to stop the coming global economic and strategic catastrophe. Meanwhile, Elliott Abrams's equally excellent "If You Will It: Rebuilding Jewish Peoplehood for the 21st Century" is also aimed at preventing a catastrophe: the future erasure of Jewish culture. His book could hardly be more timely, coming so soon after the most murderous attack on the Jewish people since the Holocaust. Mr. Abrams comes up with a number of practical suggestions that philosemites like me would urge to be undertaken while there is still time to save the world's most indispensable religion. Both books are well-researched, well-written, important, timely and above all contain realistic roadmaps to achieve two vital ends.
-- Mr. Roberts is the author of "Churchill: Walking With Destiny."
Christine Rosen
This year I endeavored to read for pleasure newer fiction in translation, which yielded gems such as "Mina's Matchbox," the new book by Yoko Ogawa (author of the wonderful "The Memory Police"), Olga Tokarczuk's "Drive Your Plow Over the Bones of the Dead," and two books by Yoko Tawada, "The Emissary" and "Paul Celan and the Trans-Tibetan Angel." Not as recent but no less compelling were Jacqueline Harpman's "I Who Have Never Known Men" and Magda Szabo's "The Door," both of which, in very different settings, explore the power of human connection and the burdens of memory. Two nonfiction books stayed with me long after finishing the last pages: Lowry Pressly's "The Right to Oblivion: Privacy and the Good Life," a remarkable and important reconsideration of our understanding of privacy in a hypermediated and information-saturated age, and Boyce Upholt's "The Great River: The Making and Unmaking of the Mississippi," an intriguing natural history that asks us to consider the unintended consequences of humans' efforts to control and engineer our world.
-- Ms. Rosen is a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute.
Ronda Rousey
Writing my second book, I came to understand the work that goes into crafting a compelling follow-up. Pierce Brown gives a masterclass in "Light Bringer," the sixth in his Red Rising series. After devouring his original trilogy years ago, I was ecstatic to learn that Mr. Brown had embarked on a four-part follow-up. (The realities of keeping up with a toddler often come at the cost of keeping up with one's reading list!) Diving back into protagonist Darrow's world was like reuniting with old friends. It came as a relief that the continuation didn't seem contrived but more like an inevitable aftermath -- it felt as if the story and characters had matured as much as I had as a reader in the intervening years. Turning the final pages of "Light Bringer," I was devastated, unready to leave the futuristic world. The only saving grace was being able to experience it again as an audiobook narrated by the incomparable Tim Gerard Reynolds. I am dying to know what happens next in "Red God," which still has no release date -- and hope the (red) god story doesn't end there.
-- Ms. Rousey is the author of "Our Fight: A Memoir."
Katherine Rundell
I love the work of Kate DiCamillo, and she had two new books out this year: "The Hotel Balzaar" and "Ferris." "Balzaar" is written in the same heightened fairy-tale note of great/excited expectation that you find in her magnificent "The Tale of Despereaux"; "Ferris" is real-world, solid, shot through with love for human frailty. Ms. DiCamillo is a conjurer: She magics generosity onto the page. Granta recently published all five volumes of Virginia Woolf's diaries, and they're a revelation. She's so sharp, so humane and piercing, at once so snobbish and so self-lacerating and so furiously intelligent. She writes about catching hold of the moment and crushing down our roaring urge to hurry: "If one does not lie back & sum up & say to the moment, this very moment, stay you are so fair, what will be one's gain, dying? No: stay, this moment. No one ever says that enough. Always hurry."
-- Ms. Rundell is the author, most recently, of "Impossible Creatures" and "Vanishing Treasures."
Ben Sasse
Some of Jonathan Haidt's critics think he's an alarmist on the subject of our kids' exposure to endless online spaces, but parents I know have read his work and responded: "Finally!" The social psychologist and NYU business professor reveals why our children -- despite, or perhaps because of, the immense prosperity in which they live -- are so intellectually and emotionally fragile. The answer he gives in "The Anxious Generation": Over three decades, we have decided to protect them far too much in the real world and far too little in the "virtual" one. After well-documented sections on the "surge of suffering" among teens, the decline of play-based childhood and its replacement by phone-based childhood, he concludes with four constructive chapters on how we can rebuild, at both the familial and societal levels. The book has generated debates and conversations across the country about how we might help the next generation to keep its sanity and grow its grit. Is there a more important topic?
-- Mr. Sasse is a former U.S. senator from Nebraska and a former president of the University of Florida.
Eric Schmidt
Lenin is said to have opined that there are decades where nothing happens and weeks when decades happen. Today it can feel like we are living through these history-making weeks on repeat. Democracy is being contested, the international order is under strain and technology is transforming society -- often all at once. Few thinkers can grasp the complexity of this moment, but Fareed Zakaria does an exceptional job. His latest book, "Age of Revolutions: Progress and Backlash From 1600 to the Present," draws on centuries of history across continents to show that, while our period may be revolutionary, it is not unprecedented. Mr. Zakaria argues convincingly that our politics are going through a fundamental shift, where the old labels of "left" and "right" no longer apply. But this is not the first such political realignment. As I read this book, I couldn't help relating his lessons to what I have argued is the most profound revolution today: the rise of artificial intelligence. Mr. Zakaria shows how the success of any revolution, including AI, is determined not by the amount of disruption it causes but by the ability of society to adapt to it and harness it for progress.
-- Mr. Schmidt is the former CEO and executive chairman of Google and the co-founder, with his wife, Wendy, of the philanthropy Schmidt Sciences, which funds science and technology research.
Kyrsten Sinema
I've long been a fan of Jonathan Haidt, requiring all my staff for the past 12 years to read "The Righteous Mind" to better understand why it's normal and OK to think differently from a friend, neighbor, family member or stranger. Mr. Haidt's latest book, "The Anxious Generation," should serve as a clarion call for all Americans. Whether you have children and grandchildren or simply care about people and the future of our society, read this book and take it seriously. Mr. Haidt shows the danger of a social-media-obsessed world for our kids' mental health and growth, makes the case for outdoor and unstructured play as beneficial for children, and offers suggestions to protect our kids' mental health and social development and our society's future.
-- Ms. Sinema is a U.S. senator from Arizona.
Barry Sonnenfeld
In the leadup to the election, with all my favorite MSNBC news pundits warning about a potential civil war, I decided to read "The Demon of Unrest," by Erik Larson, which documents the months between Abraham Lincoln's election in 1860 and his taking office, bookended by the fortification of and eventual attack on Fort Sumter. This story, a century and a half in the past, feels incredibly, unfortunately modern. Mr. Coppola, director of the "Godfather" trilogy, "The Conversation" and "Apocalypse Now," and I have nothing in common. I could never pull off his embrace of total chaos. Ruy Teixeira
We live in strange times. And surely one of the strangest aspects of them is the rise of an ideology -- call it "wokeness" -- that judges actions or arguments not by their content but by the identity of those engaging in them. Those identities in turn are defined and prioritized by an intersectional web of oppressors or oppressed, powerful or powerless, dominant or marginalized. With this approach, one does not judge actions by their effectiveness or arguments by their truth; one judges them by whether the people doing or making them are oppressed, powerless or marginalized. If so, their actions or arguments deserve support; if not, they deserve opposition. Madness! But where on earth did this madness come from and who, really, does this ideology benefit? "We Have Never Been Woke: The Cultural Contradictions of a New Elite" by Musa al-Gharbi is the book to read to understand this puzzle. Mr. al-Gharbi nails how and why the social-justice ideology of knowledge-economy professionals has risen to dominance in the name of fighting inequality and how, paradoxically, it mostly reinforces the social position of those very same professionals. Strange times indeed.
-- Mr. Teixeira the is co-author, with John B. Judis, of "Where Have All the Democrats Gone? The Soul of the Party in the Age of Extremes."
Clifford Thompson
In Eileen Kelly's novel, "Small Wonder," Tina, a single mother and preschool teacher, is not the stereotypical resident of the Park Slope neighborhood in Brooklyn, N.Y.: Surrounded by the wealthy, she has a tenuous hold on both her job and her home, and in this child-friendly neighborhood, she discovers that not all of the children are friendly -- a couple of them, in fact, are quite dangerous. One is Jonah, who is in Tina's class; the other is Jonah's older sister, Darla, a classmate of Tina's son. Tina is attracted to Patrick, whose wife -- Jonah and Darla's mother -- has just died under vague circumstances. Ms. Kelly's first novel is a funny, frightening look at grown-up confusion and the horror of childhood. In "The Mary Years," Julie Marie Wade, a writing professor and award-winning essayist and poet, uses the object of her lifelong fascination -- the fictional Mary Richards of the 1970s sitcom "The Mary Tyler Moore Show" -- as a lens through which to view her own life, from only child growing up with conservative parents in the 1990s to queer adult. A particular treat for MTM fans.
-- Mr. Thompson is the author, most recently, of "Big Man and the Little Men: A Graphic Novel," which he wrote and illustrated.
Gordon S. Wood
Although the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence is fast approaching, very few good books on the American Revolution were published this year. Perhaps this is because some academic historians no longer believe that we Americans had a revolution. According to a recent forum in the William and Mary Quarterly, the leading journal in early American history, the conventional designation "early America" should no longer be used -- it privileges the history of the United States, which, in the forum's words, "is a settler colonial project masquerading as a postcolonial polity." In other words, the colonies' break from Britain did not end colonialism in America, since the dispossession of the lands of Native Americans blatantly persisted. Consequently, the forum called for "an end to privileging U.S. independence from Britain" in our history-writing. Resisting this tide of academic darkness is Jane E. Calvert's "Penman of the Founding: A Biography of John Dickinson," a much-neglected revolutionary. Dickinson's "Letters From a Farmer in Pennsylvania" (1767-68) was the most popular pamphlet in the imperial debate leading up to the Revolution until Thomas Paine's "Common Sense" of January 1776. Although Dickinson hurt his reputation by refusing to endorse the Declaration of Independence, he nevertheless made important contributions to the creation of the United States, as Ms. Calvert makes clear in her superb biography.
-- Mr. Wood is a professor emeritus at Brown University and the author of "Power and Liberty: Constitutionalism in the American Revolution."

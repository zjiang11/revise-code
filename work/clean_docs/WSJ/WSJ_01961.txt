A generation of computing chips designed specifically for artificial intelligence could enable a host of new applications for the technology.
Some of the chips are based on a new model that mimics functions of a human brain, in which probable answers are derived through associations and patterns among available data, said Dan Hutcheson, CEO of semiconductor research firm VLSI Research.
That breaks with convention, in which chips are designed to produce answers logically and with a high degree of precision, Mr. Hutcheson said.
Most of the new AI chips consist of interconnected circuits that simultaneously conduct simple calculations to reach answers, said Jim McGregor, principal analyst at chip research firm Tirias Research.
The accuracy of the answers grows as the AI model learns. That is different from the central brain of a computer -- called the central processing unit -- which isn't designed for such math, Mr. McGregor said.
AI, around for decades, took off when graphical processing units, often useful for applications such as videogaming, were applied to AI. Now, a new generation of chips could extend the application of AI even further, VLSI's Mr. Hutcheson said.
Some new chips have neural networks that calculate probabilities so that robots and drones achieve a better contextual understanding of their environment and make smarter decisions, Mr. Hutcheson said.
"An AI chip can solve problems that a normal computer chip could take years to solve," Mr. Hutcheson said.
Cerebras Systems of Los Altos, Calif., has developed an AI chip measuring 8.5 inches square that can run neural networks on board. Placing the neural net on the chip, instead of on another component, means data doesn't have to travel as far and that information is processed more quickly, said Andrew Feldman, CEO. The chip is being used by Argonne National Laboratory in Illinois to discover drugs and vaccines for Covid-19.

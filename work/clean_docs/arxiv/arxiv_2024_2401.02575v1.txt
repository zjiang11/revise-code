LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS FOR SOCIAL NETWORKS:
APPLICATIONS, CHALLENGES, AND SOLUTIONS
Jingying Zeng, Richard Huang, Waleed Malik, Langxuan Yin, Bojan Babic,
Danny Shacham, Xiao Yan, Jaewon Yang, Qi He
Nextdoor, USA
ABSTRACT
Large Language Models (LLMs) are transforming the way people generate, explore, and engage with
content. We study how we can develop LLM applications for online social networks. Despite LLMs’
successes in other domains, it is challenging to develop LLM-based products for social networks for
numerous reasons, and it has been relatively under-reported in the research community.
We categorize LLM applications for social networks into three categories. First is knowledge tasks
where users want to find new knowledge and information, such as search and question-answering.
Second is entertainment tasks where users want to consume interesting content, such as getting
entertaining notification content. Third is foundational tasks that need to be done to moderate and
operate the social networks, such as content annotation and LLM monitoring. For each task, we
share the challenges we found, solutions we developed and lessons we learned. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive paper about developing LLM applications for social
networks.

Rejection Sampling · Natural Language Generation

Introduction
Large Language Models(LLMs), such as GPT-3 [Brown et al., 2020] and LLAMA [Touvron et al., 2023], are AI
models that can perform tasks by reading instructions. These models are pre-trained on large amounts of text corpora
and then fine-tuned to perform tasks by following the instructions [Ouyang et al., 2022]. LLMs have shown impressive
performance across different Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, and shown a great potential to adapt to unseen
tasks by in-context few-shot learning [Brown et al., 2020, Xie et al., 2021]. If we want to customize LLMs for a
specific task, we can fine-tune LLMs with labeled data in the same way we train other AI models. LLMs bring a lot of
opportunities for new AI applications due to its versatility.
In this paper, we aim to develop LLM applications for online social networks. Online social networks are the websites
where people can connect and interact to each other for different purposes. People use Facebook and Instagram to
interact with their friends, LinkedIn to connect with their colleagues, and Nextdoor to learn what is going on in their
neighborhoods. Social network sites have been developed (and incentivized) to maximize the amount of human-to-
human interactions rather than human-to-computer interactions such as human-to-LLMs. It is fascinating to imagine
how LLMs can interact with users to help them interact with other users.
We note that different social network users have different reasons for using social networks, and thus decide to define a
few use case types for LLMs and tackle each type separately. Although the primary reason may be social interactions
with other users, the goal of interactions can be different. Sometimes users want to get information or new knowledge.
For example, users would post questions like “is this restaurant good?”. Or, users want to have fun by browsing
interesting content, like watching short video clips about cats and dogs. Lastly, there are foundational tasks that need to
be done regardless of applications, such as monitoring the health of LLMs, flagging malicious content on the site and so
on. In summary, we categorize the LLM applications for social networks as follows:
• Knowledge tasks: Tasks where users want to get new knowledge or new information, such as Search among
social posts, Asking questions to other social network users;
• Engagement tasks: Tasks where we use LLMs to increase user engagement, e.g. creating interesting content
for notifications;
• Foundation tasks: This includes tasks that bring impacts across many applications horizontally. For example,
how to build to manage the API usage and LLM health, and how to build knowledge graph with LLMs belong
to this type.
Developing LLM applications for social networks is a challenging job for many reasons. First, existing LLMs are not
trained for the tasks we mentioned above. For the knowledge tasks, social network users want to know about knowledge
specific to the user’s social context, such as “which park around my home has a nice tennis court”, and the training
corpora for LLMs do not contain this kind of local, long-tail knowledge. For the engagement tasks, it is even trickier to
train LLMs because whether the content is engaging to users is highly subjective and specific to each social network.
For example, content popular among Instagram is very different from content popular among Twitter (or X) users.
Second challenge is that social networks are evolving over time while LLMs are static. For knowledge tasks, the
knowledge and information keeps changing in the social networks, but LLMs’ knowledge does not get updated after
training. For engagement tasks, users’ tastes changes dynamically over time.
Third challenge is evaluation: to evaluate LLMs’ outputs and remove hallucinations (mentioning something that is
incorrect). LLMs’ outputs are free form text which is time consuming to evaluate for human annotators.
In this paper, we discuss how to tackle the above challenges in a systematic way. These solutions come from 1-year
experience in developing several LLM applications for Nextdoor, a hyper-local social network for neighborhoods.
We found that different task types pose different challenges and need different solutions. Therefore, we will disucss
challenges and solutions for each type in the following sections. We believe that, although we present challenges and
solutions in the context of Nextdoor, these lessons are applicable to other social network settings.
Here is a summary of solutions we discuss in the paper. For knowledge tasks, we employ Retrieval Augmented
Generation (RAG) to retrieve the posts and comments from the social network that are relevant to a given task or
question. RAG addresses the first and second challenges by providing LLMs with up-to-date knowledge from the social
networks. Even with RAG, we found that the underlying LLMs make mistakes by referencing wrong content. To
address this, we fine-tune LLMs by teaching which types of answers should be preferred. We employed a fine-tuning
method that is much simpler than Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback [Ouyang et al., 2022]. For the
evaluation challenge, we adopted a LLM-based approach to evaluate the quality of LLM outputs holistically.
For the engagement tasks, we developed a reward model that predicts if the users would interact with the content made
by LLMs. We trained a reward model with our own user engagement data because each social network’s users have
4

different tastes. This reward model addresses the first challenge. To address the second challenge, we retrain the reward
model periodically. For the engagement tasks, there is no evaluation challenge because we can use user engagement
metrics.
For the foundation tasks, the above challenges do not apply. Foundation tasks are not domain specific nor time sensitive.
Evaluation is also relatively straightforward. For the foundation tasks, we discuss how we optimize the cost of LLMs
serving and how to monitor LLM performance efficiently.
Our contributions are the following:
• We give a high level overview on a broad range of LLMs applications for social networks and discuss all
possible challenges one would face.
• We share how we developed the solutions and how we productionized them end-to-end. Our solutions are
robust and scalable enough for real-world large social networks. To our knowledge, this is the first paper to
share all the details in productionizing LLMs for social network applications.

Knowledge Tasks
Knowledge tasks are fundamental in social networks since lots of users come to social network platforms for information
seeking. In this chapter, we will describe the LocalGPT system, a neighborhood-specific LLM that we build for domain-
specific question-answering.
2.1
Introduction to LocalGPT System
We build LocalGPT, a LLM that contains the local knowledge from the Nextdoor data. LocalGPT can answer users’
questions and search queries about users’ neighborhoods like Figure 1. LocalGPT can be used in search, like Microsoft
BingGPT. LocalGPT may be used to add a comment to a user’s post that has not been answered by other users.
We note that the existing LLMs like ChatGPT have shortcomings for our purposes because these models do not have up-
to-date information due to the cutoff date in the training data, and because they are not trained with questions specialized
to Nextdoor’s domain [Vu et al., 2023]. For instance, in our use case, to provide answers to the neighborhood-specific
questions such as “Does anyone know of a reputable and affordable wedding photographer in the San Francisco Bay
Area?” or “Do you have any real-time updates on the status of wildfires in Sonoma county”, pre-trained LLMs or
traditional web search might fail or provide a generic answer due to insufficient domain-specific knowledge stored or
no real-time information about the topic.
To address the limitation of existing LLMs, our LocalGPT employs two solutions. First, we apply Retrieval-Augmented-
Generation (RAG): given a user query, we retrieve the most relevant documents in the Nextdoor data, and use the
documents to generate an answer. Second, we fine-tune LLMs with our data. In particular, we fine-tune LLMs so
that they learn what questions our users would ask (instruction tuning), and also what kinds of answers the users
would prefer (preference learning). Our final models would combine these two approaches. Figure 1 is an end-to-end
illustration of the LocalGPT system.
Figure 1: An Illustration of the LocalGPT System.
Retrieval-Augmented-Generation: Our first method is RAG. In RAG, we retrieve relevant Nextdoor documents
(user’s posts and comments) for a user query. Then we ask pre-trained LLMs to generate answers using the docu-
ments [Ram et al., 2023]. RAG provides multiple benefits. First, RAG allows us to answer questions with the up-to-date
information that happened after the cutoff date of the LLM training data. Second, RAG reduces hallucination because
we ask LLMs to provide answers within the retrieved documents.
6

On the other hand, using RAG only has limitations. In some cases, the retrieved knowledge can be partially or completely
irrelevant to the given question, which makes it challenging for the pre-trained LLM to selectively use the information
in the provided context during answer generation without training. This issue is magnified especially when we use noisy
posts and associated user comments of all kinds from social network platforms to build indexes. Additionally, for time-
sensitive questions, the up-to-date knowledge retrieved and provided as in-context might contradict with the pre-trained
LLM’s prior beliefs, resulting in the model generating answers based on the out-dated knowledge rather than attending
to the new factual information in the given context [Meng et al., 2022, Hernandez et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023c].
Fine-tuning: To address the limitation of RAG, we fine-tune LLMs with the instructions related to Nextdoor’s use
cases (Instruction tuning). Our goals are the following: First, fine-tuned LLM can generate answers with higher quality
than pre-trained LLM in terms of relevance, engagingness, coherence and readability. Second, the fine-tuned LLMs
should provide useful answers even in corner cases, such as no documents retrieved or conflicting information retrieved.
Third, we use Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) instead of Reinforcement learning (RL) because production-scale APIs only
support SFT. It is a challenge to satisfy all these objectives and corner cases without using RL [Touvron et al., 2023].
To achieve the above goals, we design a novel training framework, knowledge infection training. The knowledge
injection training is a multi-objective optimization to fine-tune LLM using tasks appearing in pre-training for downstream
long-form Q&A. During the fine-tuning phase, we utilize a long-form Q&A task for knowledge injection. We also tailor
the conventional multiple choice question answering (MCQA) task as knowledge preference data to upweight the token
probability of knowledge-preferred responses, where we prefer a response generated using knowledge from our local
knowledge bank rather than the parametric knowledge learned from pre-training. Additionally, we instruction fine-tune
with RAG (IRAG) to instruct model to provide an answer by extracting factually relevant information from the provided
context and ignore the spurious correlations. We empirically observe that IRAG not only benefits the in-distribution
questions, but also enhances the performance and significantly reduces hallucination on out-of-domain questions.
Our knowledge injection training is a reinforcement-learning-free approach. The combination of tasks we use in
gradient updates creates a more deliberate attention to allow the fine-tuned LM to selectively extract and utilize the
factually related information contained in the given context for better generalization. With this training framework,
we effectively inject neighborhood-specific knowledge to off-the-shelf LMs, which mitigates hallucination during
answer generation. Our fine-tuning strategy bypasses training a reward model and utilizing reinforcement learning (RL)
techniques, providing a more direct way to adapt domain-specific knowledge and lower the factual errors in downstream
knowledge-rich tasks such as domain-specific Q&A.
To accelerate the training process and reduce the costs from human evaluation, we develop a scalable automatic
evaluation system to comprehensively evaluate the truthfulness and contextual coherence of the responses, and monitor
the negative effects that are potentially introduced by fine-tuning. This evaluation system overcomes the challenge of
the nonexistence of a benchmark dataset for our domain-specific Q&A in real-world distribution, and can be generalized
to any factoid Q&A tasks evaluation.
As a real-world Q&A application, our system design provides fundamental supports on the freshness of the knowledge
base for the models to perform in-context learning. In the retrieval system, the vector store is updated in real-time on the
creation of posts, and separated from model serving. By incorporating up-to-date knowledge retrieved from knowledge
source for conditioning generation, the LocalGPT system is able to generate more reliable answers for domain-specific
and time-sensitive questions.
The key contributions of this work are as follows:
• We build a LocalGPT system, a real-world application for domain-specific Q&A, that utilizes both fine-tuning
to endow pre-trained LLM with domain-specific knowledge and RAG architecture to enhance out-of-the-box
capabilities. The system design decouples the model serving from vector store updates to provide the fine-tuned
model with always-fresh information to answer time-sensitive questions and dynamically adapt out-dated
ground truth answers from knowledge injection training in real-time.
• We develop a multi-objective knowledge injection training framework to adapt off-the-shelf closed-source
LLMs to domain-specific Q&A task. We propose a straightforward reward-free approach to inject knowledge
and improve grounding using preference data and instruction-tuning in the training phase, which significantly
improves generalization ability and reduces hallucination during answer generation.
• For knowledge injection training, our work sheds light on the effectiveness of i) using tasks similar to pre-
training data to adapt the model to specialized downstream knowledge-rich tasks ii) using preference data,
instruction-tuning, and in-context learning to further align the LM’s attention focus with our knowledge
preference and improve knowledge grounding. The ablation studies show the effectiveness of each component
in the training framework.
7

2.2
Base Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
This section describes how we build LocalGPT without fine-tuning LLMs. In particular, we discuss using Retrieval-
Augmented-Generation (RAG). Given a user’s question, we retrieve relevant posts and comments and use LLMs to
generate an answer using the posts and comments. For model training, we will discuss it in Section 2.3.
LLMs pre-trained on public data alone does not capture neighborhood-specific knowledge as our internal data does.
However, without further training, off-the-shelf LLMs can be integrated and augmented with internally built retrieval
systems for domain-specific question-answering [Ram et al., 2023, Lewis et al., 2020]. In-context RAG model is an
industry standard approach, similar to that has been used in Microsoft Bing, and other applications. The key advantages
of this approach are that it is a fast-built, relatively cheaper, and stable approach.
Our base RAG leverages the retrieval system with the generative power of LM. It is built based on integrating a
pre-trained LLM with our internal retrieval system without any specialized training on the language model. It consists
of two sub-modules: a searched-based knowledge-retriever module that fetches the top-K of the most relevant posts and
comments via embedding-based search to provide source knowledge, and a retrieval-augmented language generator
module that leverages LLMs’ parametric knowledge with the retrieved contexts provided at inference time to produce
final answers back to users. Some research shows that the conflicts between parametric knowledge stored in LMs and
new evidence in retrieved documents affect the answer generation [Chen et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2023a]. To alleviate this
problem, we prompt LM to perform a summarization task at inference time given the relevant documents provided. For
opinion-wise questions, without explicitly asking the LM to summarize based on the given context, it may encourage
the freedom of the generated answers, which can potentially lead to some undesirable aspects. The base RAG serves
as the baseline of our LocalGPT system. With reference posts, it is easier for a human to evaluate and validate the
generated answers.
2.2.1
Implementation Details
Building Base RAG consists of three steps: data generation using the most up-to-date posts and comments, index
building through OpenAI embeddings, and connecting the retrieval system with a language generator model.
I. Data generation for Base RAG: We first collect the most recent posts and comments through our Nextdoor’s internal
database. To make the collected data a high quality and trustworthy resource for index building, we select the posts
with comments and reactions above certain thresholds. Additionally, we prepare each document as a post concatenated
with one associated thread. Each thread contains a parent post and a single comment section with respective replies
in the same thread. In this way, for each post, we are able to capture different conversation branches in the comment
section. After preprocessing, there are approximately 300K posts, branching into 1M threads for further index building.
Figure 2: System Diagram of Index Building.
8

II. Index building: In this retrieval system, the search index is built based on OpenAI embeddings. The advantage
of using OpenAI embeddings is because of its long (3k+) context window. Other embedding models (e.g 512 token
windows) are considered relatively too short to capture the entire posts and comments. In each Designated Market Area
(DMA), we create a vector embedding for every post. The user queries are embedded in the same vector space such that
Approximate Nearest Neighbors (ANN) can be used to retrieved the relevant posts.
Figure 2 is a diagram illustrating how indexes are built. We store all the posts and comments in a Postgres database
named Gnarfeed. At the time a post is created, the Task Worker calls the embedding model and stores the resulting
embedding and post ID into the vector store, where Task Worker is Nextdoor’s internal task queue system. The vector
store is built by Postgres. Postgres contains PGVector to support retrieval for related posts.
III. Connecting retrieval with language generator model: Figure 3 represents an architecture design of RAG. Given
a user question, the retrieval system returns the top-K post IDs from the vector store, PGVector database. After the
post IDs are retrieved, we then query the Gnarfeed database to get the actual post with linked threads for in-context
learning. These retrieved relevant posts and comments contribute as local knowledge to supplement the pre-trained
LLMs’ knowledge base. After prepending retrieved source to the user queries in the prompt, we use ChatGPT to
summarize and generate a response back to the user. The detailed prompt used can be found in Table 29 in the Appendix.
In this system design, we decouple the model serving from the vector store updates, since a frequent update is essential
to ensure the freshness of the posts and comments.
Figure 3: The Architecture Design of the RAG-based LocalGPT System.
However, without gradient updates, the adaptation of pre-trained LLMs on domain-specific Q&A tasks can be limited.
Therefore, in the next section, we are going to introduce our multi-objective knowledge injection training framework to
further adapt the pre-trained LLM through fine-tuning.
2.3
Multi-objective Knowledge Injection Training
Next, we are going to discuss more about the knowledge injection training framework for adapting pre-trained LLMs
with domain-specific knowledge to answer domain-specific Q&A.
2.3.1
Overview of the Knowledge Injection Training Framework
Without updating weights of pre-trained LLMs via training, research has shown a great potential of using retrieval as
an add-on tool to provide relevant source knowledge to LLMs at inference time in performing Q&A tasks. However,
it also has limitations. The RAG pipeline highly depends on the recall of the retrieval system, and the impact of
retrieval augmentation on pre-trained LLMs is still limited and does not always perform as we expect [Liu et al., 2023a].
9

An imperfect retrieval system in RAG requires the LM to generate answers conditioning on partially or completely
unrelated context. Some studies show that when relevant information appears in the middle of the retrieved source, the
performance of the LM is significantly degraded when compared to the case where the relevant information occurs
at the beginning or the end of the context [Chen et al., 2023]. LMs also tend to follow the order of the provided
in-context knowledge source, resulting in later documents contributing less than earlier documents in generating final
answers [Ivgi et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2023a]. Adapting domain-specific knowledge without training makes it hard for
LMs to recognize the irrelevant or conflicted information in the given context [ZHAO et al., 2023, Cheng et al., 2023,
Zhang et al., 2023c], and LM’s understanding of domain-specific questions can be limited since domain-specific data is
not included in the pre-training phase, which makes the LMs prone to hallucination.
On the other hand, fine-tuning pre-trained LLMs on domain-specific data provides another way to directly inject
knowledge for downstream Q&A [Singhal et al., 2023]. This knowledge injection approach through fine-tuning
is different from instruction fine-tuning [Wei et al., 2021] in the way that it emphasizes adapting domain specific
knowledge rather than fine-tuning the model to better follow instructions. Some existing work shows that LLMs’
knowledge and abilities are embedded through the pre-training phase, and one way of injecting knowledge is to continue
the pre-training process by adapting LLMs with task-specific data [Mitchell et al., 2023, Ovadia et al., 2023].
Although research on domain-adaptive pre-training has demonstrated the effectiveness of continuous pre-training on
adapting domain-specific knowledge, which enhances LLMs’ pre-trained capabilities with domain-specific knowledge
[Gururangan et al., 2020, Cheng et al., 2023], it is still uncertain if fine-tuned models’ generalization ability on the
adapted knowledge apply to all domain-specific tasks [Yunxiang et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2023]. Additionally, after
the last fine-tuning update, the knowledge of the fine-tuned model is frozen. Without having access to the most
up-to-date knowledge, the fine-tuned model along can not answer time-sensitive questions accurately.
Therefore, to leverage the strengths of both approaches, we first develop a knowledge injection fine-tuning framework
that utilizes tasks that are similar to those of the pre-training phase to effectively adapt the pre-trained LLM to domain-
specific knowledge and guide the model parameter updates towards the direction of better in-context learning; then we
augment the fine-tuned model with a retrieval system to ground the conditioning generation.
The knowledge injection training is conducted through fine-tuning on domain-specific data. In addition to injecting
knowledge through a domain-specific Q&A set, we use MCQA as knowledge preference data to encourage the model to
better align its knowledge preferences with our local knowledge base. Additionally, we instruction fine-tune with RAG
to teach the model to extract factual information from the given context when generating answers. There are two main
advantages of instruction fine-tuning with RAG. First, instruction fine-tuning can make LLMs more amenable to our
customized prompts and instructions [Chung et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2023c]. Second, since the backbone architecture
of transformer-based LLMs is attention mechanism [Vaswani et al., 2017], with proper training, such instruction-tuned
LMs can lead to a more deliberate attention mechanism to focus on the factual and relevant portions of context provided
in a given question.
The knowledge injection training can be considered as a multi-objective optimization with training objective:
L = Lft + Lprefer,
where Lft denotes fine-tuning using supervised data to adapt domain-specific knowledge and Lprefer aims to maximize
the likelihood of the appearance of domain-specific knowledge through preference alignment. At inference time, we
prompt the fine-tuned model with the user question concatenated with the retrieved source as supporting context to
ground the answer generation and dynamically adapt out-dated ground truth answers for question-answering.
Figure 4 represents an overview of the knowledge injection training framework. The training framework includes
two components: the knowledge injection through fine-tuning and the automatic evaluation system. Fine-tuning
consists of two phases. Phase I collects high quality training data from posts and comments, and converts it into raw
question-answer pairs. Phase II injects knowledge into the LLM via different tasks. Our knowledge injection training
focuses on Q&A-related tasks and instruction-following tasks since they are the most relevant to our training objective
and downstream Q&A applications. There are three tasks that we consider for knowledge adaption: long-form Q&A,
MCQA, and IRAG. MCQA serves as knowledge preference data for preference learning. IRAG is used for improving
grounding. To complete the training framework, a comprehensive evaluation system is built to assess the performance
of across different models. In this automatic evaluation system, a question generator scales up both an in-sample and an
out-of-sample question generating process. We also build a sequence of LM-based evaluators and guardrails to rate the
performance of the models and safeguard the negative effects introduced by LMs.

There are several advantages of adding retrieved source as additional context during inference. First, fine-tuning adapts
LLMs with domain specific knowledge, and additional context provided at inference time contributes to better surface
the injected knowledge embedded in the fine-tuned model, since in-context learning is essentially changing the original

to fine-tuned models that lack additional retrieval, models provided with extra factual knowledge are more grounded
and hallucinate less. Second, LLMs lack up-to-date information due to the fact that their parametric knowledge is
limited to what is provided at training time. It is infeasible to retrain LLMs hourly or weekly to answer time-sensitive
questions. Using retrieval to add the latest relevant source as context through prompt at inference time can effectively
incorporate update-to-date information in the answer generation. Third, the quality of ground truth answers in training
data generated through automatic approaches is uncertain. Since retrieval augmentation impacts an LLM’s answer
generation, adding useful in-context evidence at inference time provides a way to correct and override a fine-tuned
model’s out-dated parametric knowledge.
2.3.3
Training Set Construction Details and Rationales
We have described how the knowledge injection framework is formulated. Next, we are going to discuss the details
on how we automatically prepare training data for each task of Q&A, MCQA, and IRAG. The LM we use to prepare
training data is GPT-3.5-turbo.
Phase I: Automatic training data generation: Similar to the data collected for base RAG, the training data we use for
fine-tuning includes the most recent posts with number of comments and reactions above certain thresholds. Using
an LLM with in-context few-shot examples is a common way for generating training examples [Taori et al., 2023,
Ovadia et al., 2023]. We use ChatGPT to automatically generate raw question-answer pairs by instructing it through
the prompt listed in Table 30 in Appendix. An example of a raw question-answer pair is shown in Table 1 below, where
the answers in the pair are the golden answers. After question-answer pairs are generated, a two-stage filtering is
applied to include the pairs that are most relevant to the tasks in fine-tuning. In the first stage, the questions without a
recommendation or suggestion contained in the answer are filtered. The second stage filtering is specific to our training
data. We exclude data with special patterns. For instance, due to the nature of our data, some question-answer pairs
generated by the LM are likely to extract special patterns such as “NDXYZ 12345” from the provided posts. The
question-answer pairs containing such special forms are excluded to ensure the quality of our training data.
Phase II: Knowledge injection training: In this phase, instead of fine-tuning the LLM on domain-specific raw
question-answer corpora, we perform knowledge injection training using long-form Q&A, MCQA, and IRAG tasks.
Our approach to fine-tuning on Q&A-related tasks not only infuses domain-specific knowledge into the pre-trained
LLM, but also directly improves truthfulness of the generated answers on out-of-domain questions.

with our knowledge preference. We utilize MCQA task for preference alignment by tailoring conventional MCQA

manner, which bypasses reinforcement learning (RL) and directly optimizes the model performance via preference
learning. In the light of the training goals, we transform the raw question-answer pairs into MCQA tasks with only
factual-related questions included. The response which answers the question using local knowledge is more preferable
and is chosen as the ground truth answer. An example template of constructing user message ui for MCQA task is

Also, the following four considerations are taken into account when creating MCQA task. First, the golden answer ai in
each of the multiple choice questions contains not only the correct choice A, B, C, or D, but also the full response of the
correct answer, which improves the alignment of the generated outputs. Second, MCQA task in training data is in the
form of preferences over multiple possible answers to a question, which penalizes the answers generated using facts
from pre-trained knowledge with higher loss and encourages the model to generate an answer using the facts from
the local knowledge base. Third, we randomly replace the correct answer with one of the wrong answers to mitigate
the position bias. Fourth, when generating responses, the LM is instructed to generate a relatively short answer to the
question and we further control the length of the answers by filtering the questions with lengthy responses, since it can
be challenging for the model to maintain coherence over a long text span of words when generating an answer.
Instruction fine-tune with RAG: Achieving good generalization on out-of-domain questions can be challenging and
may lead to a severe hallucination problem. To mitigate hallucination and reduce factually inaccurate facts in the
generated answer for unseen questions, we instruction fine-tune with RAG. IRAG aims to enhance the model’s factual
predictions by instruction-tuning the model to pay closer attention to the relevant information provided in the given
context when answering the question. In other words, given a question and a supporting context that is partially or
entirely unrelated to the question, the model learns to generate more factually correct answers by extracting information
from the relevant span and ignoring the distracting surrounding text. With instruction-tuning, we are more likely
to control the fine-tuned model’s attention focus to our desire [Weston and Sukhbaatar, 2023]. When preparing the
training examples, IRAG task combines the instructions and additional context of the question into qi using system
prompt si and user message ui. The prompt template for creating IRAG task is shown in Table 3. The context we use is
the original posts and comments that create the raw question-answer pairs. For IRAG task, high-quality data is the
key. To this end, only the factual-related questions whose answers are fully supported by the context are included. In
other words, IRAG task only includes questions with answers that contain entities all extracted from the source context.

2.4
Automatic Evaluation System
Building an effective and efficient evaluation system to evaluate properties and abilities of LLMs plays an impor-
tant role in continual improvement of language models in our Q&A system. The performance of LLMs is typi-
cally evaluated based on conventional benchmarks such as SQuAD [Rajpurkar et al., 2016] and CommonSenseQA
[Talmor et al., 2018], [Mihaylov et al., 2018] to assess the capabilities in NLP tasks, common sense, and world knowl-
edge. Nonetheless, how to effectively and scalably measure the performance of knowledge injection training is still
an open question, since the off-the-shelf benchmarks do not contain domain-specific information and local factoids
to evaluate factual correctness based on the adapted knowledge. Additionally, offline evaluation for practical applica-
tions also requires simulating real user questions and evaluating open-ended answers generated by LLMs. In recent
years, LLM-based evaluators, as an alternative to human raters, has received increasing attention in both academia
and industry. The LLMs trained on instruction-following tasks are capable of following in-context instructions, and
several studies have shown the effectiveness of the LLM-based evaluators utilizing this ability in automatic evaluations
[Mitchell et al., 2023, Chiang and Lee, 2023, Liu et al., 2023d, Zeng et al., 2023b].
To evaluate the offline performance of the models, we develop a scalable end-to-end automatic evaluation system.
This automatic evaluation system overcomes the challenge of non-existence of benchmark dataset, and provides
comprehensive evaluation metrics to measure and safeguard the LM generated answers that accommodate the open-
ended nature of the simulated user questions. As seen from the high-level overview in Figure 4, the automatic evaluation
14

system consists of question generator and LM-based evaluators. This system is used in both evaluating the knowledge
injection training and comparing the performance across different models.
2.4.1
Question Generators
The performance of knowledge injection training is evaluated based on both in-sample and out-of-sample distribution
data. The in-sample distribution data is defined as the questions appearing in the training set. The in-sample questions
are used to measure the effectiveness of local knowledge adapted through knowledge injection training. The out-of-
sample distribution is defined as the unseen questions during model training. The out-of-sample questions are used
to assess the performance of the model’s generalization capabilities on real user queries and to monitor the negative
effects of LM-generated answers.
In-sample question generation: The in-sample question sets are created based on both questions randomly sampled
from the training set and paraphrased questions that are rephrased and generated by an LM. The prompt used for
paraphrasing is in Table 31 in the Appendix. The in-sample questions measure how close or divergent the LM-generated
answers are compared to the ground truth answers in the training examples. We report ROUGE-1 [Lin, 2004] for
measuring the similarity between the model output and the golden answer.
Out-of-sample question generation: The goal of out-of-sample evaluation is to evaluate the generalization abilities
in question answering and how the models respond to adversarial queries. This question generation procedure is
generalizable to any knowledge injection evaluation. Using our LocalGPT knowledge adaption training framework as
a demonstration, the out-of-sample question set is generated based on neighborhood-related factoid data containing
information about business names, business types, location information, etc. To convert atomic facts into factual
questions, the question generation process starts with a set of seed questions as the candidate pool. The seed questions
are recommendation-related and opinion-related questions. For instance, an example seed question can be “Any
recommendations on affordable after-school or extracurricular programs in Millbrae?” or “I live in San Mateo. What
do you think of Water Dog Lake Park for dog walking?”, where the underlined atomic facts can be replaced using the
neighborhood data. Specifically, we randomly sample a question from the pool of seed questions, and randomly sample
one or multiple pieces of factoid information from the neighborhood data to fill in the blank of the seed question. Also,
we use ChatGPT to paraphrase the questions in the question pool and scale up the generation process. For each of the
questions generated, we also categorize the corresponding question type for further segment analysis. The advantages
of this approach in creating out-of-sample question sets are: i) It is easy to scale up the question generation process ii)
By segmenting questions into different categories, we can compare different models’ performances across different
types of questions and detect the model performance degradation and missing knowledge more easily iii) Randomly
sampled factoid information creates questions requiring tail knowledge to provide an answer. In this way, we can test
the generalization ability of the models in a broader sense. In addition to the regular questions, we also have a separate
safety evaluation question set [Bianchi et al., 2023] including adversarial questions to probe the potential safety issues
of the models.
2.4.2
Evaluators and Guardrails
Given a question, how to automatically and quantitatively evaluate the quality of generated answers is a non-trivial task
due to the lack of a golden reference. In our evaluation system, we develop a family of evaluators and guardrails that
can systematically evaluate the performance of knowledge injection training and the quality of different LMs’ outputs
in an automatic manner. The evaluation is based on the out-of-sample question set on a variety of aspects such as
factuality, relevance, engagingness, coherence, readability, negative opinion rate, and harmful rate.
Factuality: Without human evaluation, evaluating the factuality of an LM’s output is a challenging task for knowledge
injection training because a golden dataset for assessing factualness does not exist. There are two main approaches
that are commonly used in evaluating factualness: the model’s confidence score and reference-based fact-checking
[Tian et al., 2023a]. On the one hand, using the model’s confidence score as a proxy for factuality is not practical for
closed-source LLMs. On the other hand, evaluating knowledge injection via reference-based methods can also be
challenging since the external knowledge base does not contain domain-specific information and the internal dataset
does not contain all of the world-wide facts. Simply using LM-based evaluators to evaluate the contradiction between
the retrieved source knowledge and the generated answer is not desirable since it not only highly depends on the recall
and precision of the search API, but also depends on the LM’s performance on multi-document Q&A and the position
of the relevant information provided in the retrieved source. Research has shown that when the length of an input
sequence is long, transformer-based LMs perform poorly [Poli et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2023a], which will increase the
noise of factuality evaluation since at Nextdoor, our retrieved sources are more likely to be lengthy posts concatenated
with a vast number of comments.

Our measurement of factual correctness is based on FactScore [Min et al., 2023], which measures the percentage of the
decomposed atomic facts that is supported by a golden knowledge base. Specifically, the factual correctness of our
model outputs is measured by the entity hit and precision, where entity hit is defined as the number of entities matched
with the reference and precision is defined as

LLMs can be utilized as a proxy for a human rater, and several studies have shown high correspondence between LLM
ratings and human ratings in regards to pointwise evaluation [Mitchell et al., 2023]. In this light, we use LM-based
evaluators as our automatic evaluation metrics to produce scores on different aspects of an LM generated answer,
such as relevance, engagingness, coherence, and readability. Specifically, we prompt GPT-4 by providing a rating
scale, comprehensive evaluation criteria, and step-by-step evaluation instructions to instruct it to generate a score.
The corresponding prompts used in constructing these LM-based evaluators are developed based on existing research
[Chiang and Lee, 2023] and listed in Table 32, 33, 34, 35, and 36 in the Appendix.
• Relevance: The relevance score measures how relevant an answer is for a given question on a scale of 1 to
5. A generic answer or an answer containing redundant information is considered to be less relevant than an
answer with more detailed information or multiple entities included.
• Engagingness: The engagingness score measures how engaging and attractive an answer is on a scale of 1 to
3. This dimension includes “rate-explain” to explicitly explain the rationale of each rating scale.
• Coherence: The coherence score measures if the answer is well-organized, grammatically correct, intuitive,
and easy to understand. We also instruct the LLM to align the measurement with the DUC quality question.
• Readability: The readability score measures how concise and natural an answer is. The measurement
encourages conversational-style-like answers over essay-style answers.
Pre-trained LLMs are powerful in in-context adaptation through few-shot examples [Brown et al., 2020,
Mosbach et al., 2023]. Nevertheless, there is a trade-off between helpfulness and harmlessness [Bai et al., 2022].
Even though numerous efforts have been made in both academia and industry on the safety alignment of pre-trained
LLMs to safeguard against harmful instructions, it is still unknown how much safety degradation that fine-tuning will
bring [Qi et al., 2023]. In light of this safety risk, three additional metrics are included in our evaluation system as
guardrails to evaluate the safety alignment of LMs and how they are impacted by knowledge injection training.
• Negative Opinion Rate: The negativity of an answer is assessed by the LM-based negativity evaluator,
which evaluates how many negative opinions are contained in an answer. A negative opinion is defined as an
expression of unfavorable judgment and concerns about a person, business, location, or any other topics. The
negative opinion rate computes the percentage of the generated answers having a negativity score over 70.
• Harmful Rate: When computing the harmful rate, we first prompt the LM to generate responses to the
adversarial question set, and then we calculate the percentage of the generated answers that do not comply
with OpenAI’s Moderation safeguard [OpenAI, 2023c].
• Socially Biased Content Rate: This metric computes the percentage of outputs containing content related to
particular demographics or sensitive information like age, gender, race, or politics that could potentially raise
concerns about potential misuse. Since LLMs themselves can be socially biased [Sap et al., 2019], we use
human evaluation for these metrics.

Other lessons learned: We also try inject knowledge through Yes or No factoid questions such as “Question: Is
{restaurant A} a restaurant in Palo Alto?” in the hope that when a related restaurant recommendation question is asked,
the model should be able to answer by recognizing that {restaurant A} is a potential candidate within the area. However,
what we find is that these factoid Q&As do not help the fine-tuned model generalize the recommendation through its
reasoning ability, but rather they get the fine-tuned model confused. Adding more training examples with “Yes” or “No”
as ground truth answers influences the answer generation and significantly increases the probability of the predicted
token being “Yes” or “No”. As a result, when prompting the model with a question asking for recommendation, the
model only produces the responses “Yes” or “No” without explanation. This failure of generalization through reasoning
is expected due to the reversal curse of LLMs [Berglund et al., 2023].
2.6
Deployment Considerations
We use the following principles to guide our architecture design of the system, availability, low latencies, and real-time
updates. Availability requires LLM models have uptime of 99.95%. To this end, we use Databricks Serving to leverage
the index size and productivity gains. To ensure low latencies after adopting RAGs architecture, we introduce a
multistage model that retrieves candidate posts from a vector store and subsequently calls the OpenAI API for the
GPT or fine-tuned model. Also, we decouple the vector store update from model serving to ensure the freshness of the
in-context information provided for time-sensitive Q&A. The embeddings of the posts are created in real-time at post
creation and stored into vector storage database.

We show that combining RAG architecture with a fine-tuned model trained on domain-specific knowledge and context
exhibits great potential in real-time question-answering applications. The LocalGPT system enriches the LLM’s
parametric knowledge with our domain-specific knowledge base. In the system design, decoupling model serving from
frequent vector store updates provides a foundation for the freshness of the retrieved knowledge source. Using the
up-to-date retrieved knowledge in-context makes it possible to answer time-sensitive Q&A and dynamically adapt
the ground truth answers from knowledge injection training. From the modeling perspective, we build an end-to-end
training framework and demonstrate the effectiveness of knowledge injection training for downstream Q&A that
requires local knowledge. Through knowledge injection training, the fine-tuned LM learns to emphasize the relevant
and factual knowledge from the given context when performing in-context learning, which significantly increases
generalization capability and reduces factual errors. We also find that relevant and up-to-date in-context documents have
a bigger influence on retrieval augmented fine-tuned models than on pre-trained LLMs in terms of surfacing factual
answers. Our empirical studies show that at inference time, when the retrieved source is not relevant and the injected
knowledge does not contain relevant information either, the fine-tuned model benefits from its pre-trained knowledge.
23

Limitation and future work: Even though the fine-tuned model with retrieval generates better answers in domain-
specific questions and improves factual grounding based on our multi-dimensional evaluation, there are still challenges
and limitations that remain unsolved. Retrieved knowledge provided in-context mitigates the issues of hallucination
associated with large language models (LLMs). However, we still can not fully prevent hallucination, particularly for
cases when no parametric knowledge exists and no relevant documents are retrieved. Also, fine-tuning compromises
the safety alignment of pre-trained LLMs; thus adding blocklist or additional detection will be essential to filter out
malicious instructions. There is a lot of opportunity for the LocalGPT system to be further improved. For instance, the
latency introduced by OpenAI requests degrades the performance on real-time Q&A. We can explore open-sourced
models for more customized training to reduce the dependency on closed-sourced LLMs. Secondly, the location
targeting issue for the recommendation-related questions can be improved by increasing the recall of the retriever.
Re-ranking and filtering before passing to an LLM for final response generation can also be an alternative in improving
the accuracy of the retrieval system. From the fine-tuning perspective, scaling-up the diversity of the tasks and the size
of the training example can potentially improve the generalization ability of the model. Also, more variety of preference
data can be used to align the style and tone of the generated outputs with our user preferences. Last but not least, better
prompting strategies on how to better incorporate LLMs with external knowledge in-context are critical to improving
the model performance. We leave the exploration of these ideas to future investigations.
2.8
Related Work
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG): RAG architecture with in-context learning has been widely used to
endow pre-trained LLMs with domain-specific knowledge. Without updating model weights via gradient descent,
one can leverage proprietary LLMs’ parametric knowledge with relevant retrieved knowledge in-context to enhance
the generation process [Ram et al., 2023]. Even though augmenting LLMs with retrieval systems without further
training has been demonstrated as an effective way to incorporate external knowledge and reduce the rate of generating
ungrounded concepts, it is still uncertain to what extent the retrieved knowledge source, when provided in-context,
can impact the LLM’s answer generation [Wang et al., 2023b, Chen et al., 2023], and how efficiently the LLM can
distinguish between relevant and irrelevant information in the context provided [Shi et al., 2023]. There have been
numerous studies investigating different ways of incorporating retrieved knowledge into an LM’s answer generation
process, either through training or better prompting strategies at inference time. Several methods have been proposed
to jointly train retriever and language models [Guu et al., 2020, Lewis et al., 2020, Izacard et al., 2022], and then
incorporating additional components such as an extra summarization model [Yoran et al., 2023] or a self-reflection
mechanism [Asai et al., 2023] to filter out irrelevant retrieved passages. Several authors utilize the properties of cross-
attention mechanisms in transformer-based LLMs to build RAG systems [Borgeaud et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2022].
Also, some literature advocates for using passive multi-time retrieval [Yao et al., 2022, Trivedi et al., 2022] or active
multi-time retrieval based on a model’s own confidence scores [Jiang et al., 2023] to improve the performance of
retrieval in retrieval-augmented LMs.
Knowledge Injection Training: Training LLMs on domain-specific corpora is a demonstrated and effective approach
for integrating domain knowledge into the models for downstream specialized tasks. Training from scratch is one
way of enhancing LLMs in domain-specific tasks, and BloombergGPT is one of the successful models trained using
this approach [Wu et al., 2023]. On the other hand, some research shows that continued pre-training or fine-tuning
of LLMs is more cost-efficient and leads to better generalization than training from scratch [Radford et al., 2019,
Cheng et al., 2023]. Extensive literature has developed in regards to an instruction fine-tuning approach to align
pre-trained LLMs to specific-domains such as medicine [Xiong et al., 2023, Singhal et al., 2022, Singhal et al., 2023]
and law [Huang et al., 2023]. More recently, some researchers have proposed adapting LLMs to domain-specific tasks
via continued pre-training on domain-specific data transformed into reading comprehension tasks [Cheng et al., 2023]
or multiple-choice datasets [Ovadia et al., 2023]. Broadly speaking regarding LLM adaptation, some studies investigate
how to efficiently tailor models for task-specific objectives. some representative work on parameter-efficient fine-tuning
are LoRA [Hu et al., 2021], AdaLoRA [Zhang et al., 2023b], and Llama-adapter [Zhang et al., 2023d]. Despite the
promising results observed in various downstream NLP tasks when adapting local knowledge through training, the
parametric knowledge injected does not receive further updates once the models are trained [Vu et al., 2023]. This
limitation results in LLMs lacking up-to-date knowledge to effectively answer real-time questions. Our system design
provides a foundation of real-time updates to the knowledge base so that the fine-tuned LM can utilize both knowledge
injected through training and real-time knowledge provided through the prompt to ground its answer generation.
Hallucination and Factuality Training: Hallucination has been identified as a big challenge in building trustworthy
LMs [Zhang et al., 2023a]. A series of studies has been focusing on detecting factually incorrect claims in LM-generated
output. The work can be classified into two categories: reference-based that compares the generated responses to
external knowledge resources [Chern et al., 2023, Min et al., 2023] and uncertainty-based that utilizes the model’s
own confidence scores to evaluate factualness [Manakul et al., 2023]. Other researchers investigate the importance of
24

inference-time interference in reducing factual errors. Meta AI proposes asking LLMs to self-verify its own answer to
reduce hallucination [Dhuliawala et al., 2023]. DoLa’s approach proposes a decoding algorithm by contrasting layers
using next-token distribution [Chuang et al., 2023]. However, directly optimizing factuality through fine-tuning is still
an underexplored area. Some existing works employ preference data in training to align model outputs with factuality
preferences [Tian et al., 2023b, Zhang et al., 2023f].
Aligning LLMs with Human Preference: Preference alignment has been an area of active research in both academia
and industry. Preference alignment seeks to align LLMs behaviors with human preference. Prominent approaches
to tailor an LLM’s behavior with human feedback is through instruction-tuning [Wei et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022]
and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) [Casper et al., 2023]. The RLHF technique has been widely
employed in training state-of-the-art LLMs, such as GPT-4 [OpenAI, 2023b], Llama2 [Touvron et al., 2023], and
Bard [Google, 2023]. Reinforcement learning algorithms, such as PPO [Schulman et al., 2017], can significantly
improve an LLM’s NLG ability. However, RLHF also introduces complexity into learning pipeline and increases
computational costs [Rafailov et al., 2023]. As an alternative, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with preference data is
also an effective way to incorporate human preference in model training. Recent work such as imitation learning
[Campos and Shern, 2022, Scheurer et al., 2023], SLiC-HF [Zhao et al., 2023], and DPO [Rafailov et al., 2023] have
shown competitive results in optimizing model behavior using preference data without explicitly training reward models.
Automatic evaluation: LM-based evaluators have been demonstrated as valuable tools in automatically measuring
the quality of the LLM-generated content. One notable characteristic of these reference-free evaluators is their ability
to exhibit consistency and high correspondence with human judgments [Chiang and Lee, 2023, Zeng et al., 2023b,
Zhang et al., 2023e]. Existing work shows that GPT-3 and GPT-4 have proven efficiency and reliability in both
point-wise and pair-wise evaluations [Mitchell et al., 2023, Tian et al., 2023c], they and have been widely used in
natural language generation (NLG) evaluation [Liu et al., 2023c, Fu et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023a]. In recent years, a
series of research has been conducted on finding prompting strategies to improve the performance of LLM evaluators
[Liu et al., 2023c, Chiang and Lee, 2023, Zheng et al., 2023]. [Chan et al., 2023, Li et al., 2023] suggests building an
evaluation system using multiple LLMs to communicate the evaluation results and collaboratively evaluate generated
outputs on open-ended questions.

Engagement Tasks
Besides knowledge tasks, social networks also aim to increase meaningful interaction and connectivity among users. In
this chapter, we study how we strive to increase user engagement in social networks using Generative AI. We build on

3.1.1
Motivation
Continuously generating customized and engaging content through different channels plays a pivotal role in enhancing
user engagement on social networks. Large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable proficiency in content
generation through “in-context learning”, providing a scalable and convenient way to generate personalized content for
diverse users.
However, an informative and well-summarized content generated by pre-trained LLMs does not necessarily lead to an
increase on user engagement metrics, such as click through rates (CTR). It is mainly due to the following reasons. First,
the pre-trained LLMs are not trained specifically to optimize user engagement. User tastes also vary from different
social network platforms. Even though in-context learning provides a simple way to instruct pre-trained LLMs through
prompts to generate task specific content, the performance on boosting user engagement related metrics still reaches
bottleneck. Second, the quality of the LLM-created content is not guaranteed. The content can be too generic or suffers
from hallucination, which makes it less appealing to users.

of their appearing. The probability of predicting the token “Yes” is used as the reward score. For each post,
the reward model computes the reward scores for both rule-based and ChatGPT-generated subject lines. The
ChatGPT-generated subject lines will be served to the users only when the reward model approves.

3.1.4
Deployment consideration
We have end-to-end deployed the system for nearly 85M Nextdoor users. We post-process the ChatGPT-generated
subject lines by applying a word limit of 10. There are two reasons why we choose 10 as the maximum length. First,
based on the A/B experiment result of comparing different length limits, 10 is the optimal value. Second, even if we
specify the maximum length in the prompt of the GPT-based subject line generator, it does not always follow the
instructions as we desire.
Additionally, we alleviate the rate limit issues and reduce the operational costs by caching the results from the models.
With caching, we significantly reduce the number of tokens and the number of requests send to OpenAI API.
Last but not least, we implement a daily monitoring system to monitor the performance of the models. Pairwise user
preference data is collected from a small traffic of two user buckets. The control group uses rule-based generator to
generate subject lines while the treatment group uses GPT-generated subject lines. The ground truth is collected based
on the user click data. We monitor the predictive accuracy of the reward model. If the predictive accuracy decreases by
10%, we retrain the reward model using the newly collected data.

Foundation Tasks
So far, we have discussed knowledge tasks and entertainment tasks. In this chapter, we will discuss the third type of
task, “foundation” tasks, that does not belong to a specific kind of application. In particular, we discuss the construction
of Knowledge Graphs in section 4.1 and the development of a centralized API for calling LLMs in section 4.2.
4.1
Local Knowledge Graph Construction
4.1.1
Motivation
Having more local knowledge provides Nextdoor with a competitive edge in local markets against peers. Local
knowledge graphs can be constructed through the creation of standardized content data from unstructured user generated
content (UGC) so as to provide a better understanding of neighbors and content on the platform. “Standardized” means
that there is a ground truth for the data so that we can build machine learning solutions that can predict the correct label
for a given task. In order to create machine learning models that infer different facets about UGC, we need to first
develop a “golden dataset” made up of content that is currently labeled. For example, we may want to label the topic of
a post (e.g. home services, emergency alert, nature photo), the tone (e.g. cheerful, concerned, angry) or the objective
(e.g. getting advice, searching for recommendation, rallying support).
Collecting high-quality labeled data is usually a time consuming and expensive task, as it requires the development of a
taxonomy and training human annotators or using human-in-the-loop machine learning platforms to correctly apply
labels against a taxonomy. Using domain experts in data labeling process ensures the annotations are consistent and of
high-quality; however, creating such accurate labels is an expensive and slow process.
Considering the trade-off among time, quality, and cost, semi-supervised learning [Learning, 2006], also called weak
supervision, provides an alternative for data annotation tasks. Semi-supervised learning uses a combination of a small
amount of human-labeled data usually used in supervised learning followed by a large amount of unlabeled data that is
commonly used in an unsupervised learning. This approach annotates data with reasonable time and cost, but due to the
unsupervised learning aspect, the data created is inevitably noisy. Table 20 summarizes the comparison among the three
mentioned methods for data annotation.

In recent years, transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success in different tasks
through in-context learning. Giving a set of clear and concise instructions through prompts, expensive LLMs, such
as GPT-4, can be used as near-human annotators to create a collection of labeled data across different domains. Even
though using LLMs as human annotators yields accurate and cheaper-than-human labels, the annotation costs are still
high due to the extensive calls from expensive proprietary LLMs.
Knowledge distillation, also known teacher-student learning, was introduced to train a smaller student model to imitate
the behavior of a larger and more complex teacher model [Hinton et al., 2015]. To further reduce operational costs, we
can transfer knowledge from a large LM to a student model by supervised fine-tuning a smaller LM using seed labels
created by the most expensive LLMs.
4.1.2
Teacher-student Annotation Framework
In this light, we propose a reliable, scalable and cost-saving solution using this teacher-student framework to scale
up the data labelling process. As can be seen from Figure 10, this data labeling process consists of two stages. The
first stage employs an expensive LLM, GPT-4, in a few-shot manner as teacher-annotator. To build a good teacher
annotator, we involve a human-in-the-loop to manually review the labels created by the LLMs. In this stage, we
iterate between GPT-4 and human annotators to i) improve the labeling guidelines for evaluation in order to increase
inter-agent-agreement ii) refine the prompts used by GPT-4 with the goal of improving the label quality through
iterations. At the end of this iterative process, we collect human-reviewed labels and further supervised fine-tune
(SFT) a cheaper model, GPT-3.5, as our student-annotator. The second stage represents the data annotation pipeline
in production. In this stage, we use the fine-tuned model to perform data labeling at scale. This two-stage process
31

can be easily repeated across different domains to effectively automate the data labelling process, which serves as the
foundation of the knowledge graph’s construction pipeline.

4.1.3
Experiment
We evaluate this framework on the task of classifying posts into different categories based on time sensitivity. Classifying
user posts according to their time sensitivity is important since we can use it to find the optimal time to notify users and
show the content.
The time sensitivity is defined as a set of mutually exclusive categories, each describing the time a content can be read
or distributed. We introduce 4 distinct time sensitivity categories that should cover all types of content:
• Immediate/Urgent: This category includes content that requires immediate attention or action. It relates
to time-critical information or situations that demand urgent response or assistance. Examples could be
emergency alerts, safety warnings, or real-time updates on ongoing events.
• Time-bound/Upcoming: This category comprises content that has a specific timeframe or deadline associated
with it. It pertains to events, promotions, or activities scheduled to occur in the near future. This category helps
notify users about time-limited opportunities or important dates. Examples include event announcements,
limited-time offers, or registration deadlines. The content encourages immediate action before the specified
cutoff point.
• Evergreen/Timeless: This category refers to content that remains relevant and valuable regardless of the
timing. It contains information that does not have an expiration date and can be useful to users over an extended
period. Evergreen content often includes tips, educational resources, or general knowledge that is not tied to
specific timeframes.
• Ongoing/Recurring: This category covers content that is continuously applicable and relevant. It includes
updates, reminders, or announcements about ongoing initiatives, regular activities, or services that are
consistently available. This type of content maintains its relevance over an extended period and often requires
periodic updates. Examples could be a regular farmers market, fitness classes, or neighborhood watch meetings.
From an ML perspective, the user post classification problem can be formulated as multi-class classification where for
each post, the classifier can assign one label where the labels correspond to use cases. For each of the labels, the model
32

assigns a score between 0 and 1. Then we decide the winning category given the score. The objective of the model is to
predict the probability of a content belong to a certain time sensitivity category, given all the features.

4.2
Centralized API for LLMs
Motivation At Nextdoor, there are many LLM-related features and experiments running online and offline at any given
time. To manage the variety of ongoing LLM projects at Nextdoor, we implement a centralized API for interacting with
LLMs. This API aims to enable the following key functionalities.
Access governance: In order for a project to call OpenAI in an online setting, the project must check-in an experiment
configuration in code. This configuration contains the experiment name, the name of the team owning the experiment,
the expected experiment cost and budget, the expected queries per second, and the max tokens outputted by OpenAI.
Using this information, we create dashboards and monitors by model and by experiment. We also maintain a blocklist
that enables us to shut down an experiment on the fly as a preventative measure.
Budget management: Since all of our calls to OpenAI are in a single endpoint, we can gather metrics for all of our
calls in real time. These metrics are logged in DataDog. We track token usage and cost metrics per experiment and
create automated alerts on top of these metrics.
Ability to make generalized improvements: In the centralized API, we wrap the OpenAI call with various functionali-
ties depending on the use case. For retry-able OpenAI requests, we utilize the tenacity package to retry any flaky errors
coming from OpenAI. For offline batch requests, we leverage multiprocessing to make OpenAI requests in parallel and
circumvent the high latency. The organization-level OpenAI rate limit per model should be set high enough to enable
this, and the parallelism should be set such that the rate limit isn’t triggered.
Accessiblity from different services: We proxy OpenAI requests through Adroit, our in-house ML serving platform.
Adroit runs on Flask and is deployed via Kubernetes. Every serve-able model has an explicit REST endpoint. We create
an additional endpoint to serve OpenAI proxy requests. Adroit clients already exist in the different services within
Nextdoor, and thus we can also leverage past and future optimizations to latency and cost optimizations (e.g. request
routing, container specifications, etc.).
Future Work: We do not yet have a good solution for using OpenAI fine-tuned models in different high QPS use
cases. We have high priority OpenAI requests coming from an online setting serving real-time traffic, and we also
have batches of low priority OpenAI requests coming from an offline setting due to offline experiments. Developing a
priority-aware queuing system for LLM requests will be interesting future work.

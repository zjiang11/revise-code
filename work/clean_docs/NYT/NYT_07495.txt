Movies
Ratings and running times are in parentheses; foreign films have English subtitles. Full reviews of all current releases: nytimes.com/movies.
* 'Amour' (PG-13, 2:07) A masterpiece about life, death and everything in between, the latest from the great director Michael Haneke ("The White Ribbon," "Caché") takes a long, hard, tender look at an elderly French couple, Georges and Anne -- played by two titans of French cinema, Jean-Louis Trintignant and Emmanuelle Riva -- in their final days. Bring hankies. (Manohla Dargis)
* 'Argo' (R, 2:00) A bearded Ben Affleck directed, and stars in, this smart, jittery thriller about a real if stranger-than-fiction chapter of the Iranian hostage crisis that brought together a smart American intelligence officer, some plucky Canadians and two jaundiced Hollywood veterans (the funny tag team of John Goodman and Alan Arkin) to save the day. (Dargis)
* 'Beasts of the Southern Wild' (PG-13, 1:33) A blast of joyful, chaotic emotion from the bayou, Benh Zeitlin's dazzling film is a homegrown magic-realist fable of defiance in the face of hardship. A community in a stretch of coastal Louisiana called the Bathtub is menaced by storms and government benevolence. Their ordeal is narrated by a young girl named Hushpuppy (Quvenzhané Wallis), who lives with her father, Wink (Dwight Henry), and who belongs in the tradition of Huck Finn, Scout Finch, Eloise and other indomitable American child heroes. (A. O. Scott)
* 'Beyond the Hills' (No rating, 2:30, in Romanian) The Romanian director Cristian Mungiu ("4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days") uses the real-life case of an exorcism gone wrong at a rural convent to weave a complicated, subtle and horrifying story of faith, friendship and cruelty. (Scott)
* 'The Call' (R, 1:34) Brad Anderson directs this effectively creepy thriller -- a solid, B-movie-style exploitation flick -- about a 911 operator (Halle Berry) and the young miss in peril (Abigail Breslin) who calls for help from the inside the trunk of a car. (Dargis)
'Dead Man Down' (R, 1:50) Colin Farrell plays a thug with a secret past who meets up with a disfigured woman (Noomi Rapace) seeking revenge in a would-be thriller that is too innocuous to qualify as either actually good or delectably bad. (Dargis)
* 'Django Unchained' (R, 2:45) Blaxploitation and spaghetti western meet in the Old South in Quentin Tarantino's latest provocation, with -- literally! -- explosive results. Jamie Foxx is Django, a slave freed by a German bounty hunter (Christoph Waltz), whose partner and protégé he becomes. After the two kill off a bunch of bad guys, they set off to free Django's wife (Kerry Washington) from captivity at a nightmarish plantation called Candyland. Bloody, farcical and cartoonish, "Django Unchained" is at once a wildly entertaining, borderline-irresponsible fantasy and a bold and serious attempt to use the imaginative resources of popular culture to address an old and durable historical injustice. (Scott)
'Emperor' (PG-13, 1:38) Tommy Lee Jones's portrayal of Gen. Douglas MacArthur is the only spark that lights this dull, plodding drama about the relationship between the United States and Japan and the fate of the Japanese emperor Hirohito immediately after World War II. (Stephen Holden)
'Escape From Planet Earth' (PG, 1:29) They're not Smurfs, but the animated beings in this children's movie are blue. A pair of them, feuding brothers, end up trapped on Earth by an evil general and find that teamwork is the key to getting home. It's predictable and largely forgettable stuff, but a harmless enough winter diversion. (Neil Genzlinger)
* '56 Up' (No rating, 2:24) The latest installment in Michael Apted's remarkable documentary project that has followed a group of Britons since 1964, starting when they were 7, checks in on them at 56. (Dargis)
'From Up on Poppy Hill' (PG, 1:31) This charming animated feature from Ghibli Studios takes a nostalgic look back at Yokohama in 1963, when two teenagers meet, fall in love, and discover that they share family secrets dating back to the war. (Scott)
* 'The Gatekeepers' (PG-13, 1:37) Not recommended for anyone clinging to optimism about the Middle East, this documentary, consisting of interviews with six former heads of Shin Bet, the Israeli security agency, is a devastating and informative anatomy of politics, terrorism and the post-1967 collisions between Zionism and Palestinian nationalism. Recommended for anyone who can handle the truth. (Scott)
'Ginger & Rosa' (PG-13, 1:30) Sally Potter surveys the somewhat familiar landscape of early-'60s coming-of-age with lyricism and ferocity. Ginger (Elle Fanning) and Rosa (Alice Englert), teenagers who have been friends since birth, grapple with nuclear anxiety, sexual curiosity, and the enigmas and disappointments of the older generation. Ms. Potter's sense of the cultural moment and the social milieu (bohemian London) is acute, as is her sensitivity to the vicissitudes of female friendship. The story at times swings wildly toward melodrama, but the performances of the two young leads -- Ms. Fanning's in particular -- are strong and precise. (Scott)
'A Good Day to Die Hard' (R, 1:37) The fifth installment in this punchy action franchise takes John McClane (Bruce Willis) to Moscow for a sentimental reunion with his son (Jai Courtney) and many violent confrontations with bad guys. The scrappy wit that made the first "Die Hard" survives mainly in Mr. Willis's facial expressions, which are small compensation for enduring a dumb, frantic and depressingly uninspired episode in blowing stuff up. (Scott)
'Hava Nagila (The Movie)' (No rating, 1:15) Apart from a few missteps (including a too-cutesy narration) this buoyant documentary does a good job of looking at a chunk of recent Jewish history through the unlikely prism of a sometimes despised, sometimes loved cultural artifact: "Hava Nagila." (And sorry if it's now stuck in your head.) (Rachel Saltz)
'Identity Thief' (R, 1:47) In this lazy, generic, sporadically amusing odd-couple comedy directed by Seth Gordon, the invaluable Melissa McCarthy plays an unrepentant thief who wreaks havoc on a milquetoast (Jason Bateman) by stealing his credit cards and more. The two stars help ease the pain. (Dargis)
'The Incredible Burt Wonderstone' (PG-13, 1:40) The Steve Carell comedy about a Las Vegas magician can't transcend the fundamental problem built into movies and television shows about prestidigitation. Magic isn't really magic if you're watching it secondhand. (Holden)
'Jack the Giant Slayer' (PG-13, 1:55) The makers of this painlessly diverting movie starring Nicholas Hoult embellish the familiar bedtime story with 3-D and other effects, noisy battles and an occasional wink at the material. (Dargis)
'Koch' (No rating, 1:34) Neil Barsky's portrait of Edward I. Koch, the three-term New York mayor (who died on Feb. 1, the day the movie opened), is affectionate but not uncritical. The film moves back and forth between a condensed history of Mr. Koch's time in office and visits with him in the fall of 2010, as he campaigns for various candidates, defends his record and revisits old grudges. A personality this large and complicated may require more than 94 minutes, but this documentary is catnip for the nostalgic and the curious. (Scott)
'The Last Exorcism, Part II' (PG-13, 1:28) Poor Nell (Ashley Bell) thought she was done with Abalam, the demon who impregnated her in this horror franchise's first outing. But no. Now she's in a New Orleans halfway house for trauma victims, and her tormentor comes a-calling anew. The scares are routine, but Ms. Bell and the appealing newcomer Julia Garner enhance the proceedings immeasurably. (Andy Webster)
'Leviathan' (No rating, 1:27) Life on a commercial fishing trawler is rendered with surreal, sometimes bewildering vividness in this immersive documentary by Lucien Castaing-Taylor and Véréna Paravel. (Scott)
'Life of Pi' (PG, 2:06) In bringing Yann Martel's beloved novel to the screen, Ang Lee has created a 3-D digital fable that is often stunning to behold. The middle section, in which the title character and a Bengal tiger share a lifeboat after a shipwreck, is touching and clever, but its delicacy is spoiled by a fuzzy, cloying spiritualism that hangs over everything like a humid cloud. (Scott)
* 'Lore' (No rating, 1:48, in German) The title character memorably played by Saskia Rosendahl is the abandoned 14-year-old daughter of Nazi parents herding her younger siblings through the Bavarian woods at the end of World War II. The movie plays like a dark fairy tale. (Holden)
'Mama' (PG-13, 1:49) This ghost story starring Jessica Chastain offers a strong sense of time and place, along with the kind of niceties that don't often figure into horror flicks, notably pictorial beauty, an atmosphere throbbing with dread and actors so good that you don't want anyone to take an ax to them. (Dargis)
'Mindless Behavior: All Around the World' (No rating, 1:26) Boy bands live again in the R&B-lite foursome of the title, seemingly assembled in a test tube to sell records for their producer-Svengali, Walter W. Millsap III. This documentary follows them on tour, as the talented teenagers induce swoons and screams from their largely adolescent and preadolescent female fans. As for the documentary's purpose, it too seemingly exists to sell records for Mr. Millsap. (Webster)
'Les Misérables' (PG-13, 2:37) Tom Hooper ("The King's Speech") directs this overly busy screen version of the popular musical with Hugh Jackman as the reformed prisoner, Russell Crowe as his tormentor and Anne Hathaway as the prostitute turned martyr. By the grand finale, when tout le monde is waving the French tricolor in victory, you may instead be raising the white flag in exhausted defeat. (Dargis)
* 'No' (R, 1:58, in Spanish) In Pablo Larraín's sly, smart, fictionalized tale about the art of the sell during a fraught period in Chilean history, advertising isn't only an art; it's also a way of life. That's certainly true for the young adman, René (Gael García Bernal), who signs on to a campaign to oust Gen. Augusto Pinochet. René may be vaguely interested in selling the country on life without Pinochet, but what reels him in is the challenge of pitching a superior product. (Dargis)
'On the Road' (R; 2:04) The Brazilian director Walter Salles's film of Jack Kerouac's "On the Road," is pungent and picturesque and has a sensational jazz soundtrack, but its lead male roles, especially that of Kerouac, are sadly miscast. (Holden)
'Oz the Great and Powerful' (PG, 2:10) Save your money, spare yourself the headache and watch "The Wizard of Oz" again instead. One day, when you will catch a few minutes of this "Oz" on cable, you will shudder at its horror and you will thank me. (Dargis)
* 'Reality' (R, 1:56, in Italian) In his latest, the director Matteo Garrone ("Gomorrah") follows a fictional fishmonger who tries to land a spot on Italy's version of "Big Brother." The story is about one man's desire to make it big on the small screen, and something of a familiar exploration of the blurring between reality and its simulations. More elliptically and more interestingly, it is also a look at an Italy engrossed with rituals and spectacle, in watching and being watched. (Dargis)
'Safe Haven' (PG-13, 1:55) Watching this machine-tooled romantic thriller, the eighth film adaptation of a Nicholas Sparks novel, set in a North Carolina shore town, is like drawing a scented bath. The first spritz from the faucet may be scalding, but once you adjust the water temperature, you can settle back into the weepy sudsfest and dream the impossible dream. (Holden)
* 'Searching for Sugar Man' (PG-13, 1:26) There are several entrancing mysteries circulating in Malik Bendjelloul's hugely appealing documentary about fans, faith and an enigmatic Age of Aquarius musician known as Rodriguez, who burned bright and hopeful before disappearing. Don't wait for the DVD. (Dargis)
* 'Side Effects' (R, 1:55) This chilly, twisty thriller -- which Steven Soderbergh has said will be his last feature, at least for a while -- changes direction several times. A young woman suffering from depression (the spooky Rooney Mara) does something terrible, apparently under the influence of a new antidepressant. Her sympathetic psychiatrist (Jude Law) tries to figure out what happened, and by the time he does the context of the crime has passed from corporate conspiracy to everyday lust and greed. Mr. Soderbergh is a skillful orchestrator of moods and effects, with a sharp eye for the telling details and uncomfortable contradictions of modern life. (Scott)
'The Silence' (No rating, 1:51, in German) This grim, effective German thriller, directed by Baran bo Odar, examines the impact of a pair of eerily similar crimes, committed 26 years apart, on the police, the possible perpetrators and the families of the victims. (Scott)
* 'Silver Linings Playbook' (R, 2:00) David O. Russell's exuberant new comedy tracks the feverish, happy, sad, absurdly funny ups and downs of a head case (Bradley Cooper) just as he returns to his parents' home after eight months in a mental institution. When he meets a heartbreak beauty (Jennifer Lawrence), he finds fresh purpose in a movie that honks for happiness. (Dargis)
'Snitch' (PG-13, 1:35) For all its violence and road rage, this thriller starring Dwayne Johnson, a k a the Rock, who is too big for the movie, doesn't disintegrate into noisy popcorn nonsense. The supporting performances of Susan Sarandon and Barry Pepper further help sustain a ground-level realism. (Holden)
* 'Spring Breakers' (R, 1:34) In his latest, the pasticheur and cultural vulgarian Harmony Korine, takes four beauties, including the former Disney teen queens Selena Gomez and Vanessa Hudgens, to Florida on spring break. There, they meet James Franco in a film that, from one angle, comes across as a savage social commentary that skitters from one idea to another, including white faces, black masks, celebrity, the American dream, the limits of self-interest and the search for an authentic self. From another, it comes off as the apotheosis of the excesses it so spectacularly displays. (Dargis)
'Stoker' (R, 1:40) In his first English-language feature, Park Chan-wook, South Korea's master of bloody, elegant vengeance, explores the lurid psychodramas of an aristocratic Connecticut family. Mia Wasikowska plays India Stoker, a brooding, sensitive teenager living with her mother (Nicole Kidman) and a previously unknown uncle (Matthew Goode) who shows up after India's father dies. At a certain point the film tips from haunting weirdness to preposterous melodrama, but along the way it provides an enjoyable, unnerving jolt of cruel, Gothic sensationalism. (Scott)
'21 & Over' (R, 1:33) In this comedy, written and directed by Jon Lucas and Scott Moore ("The Hangover"), two longtime pals (Miles Teller and Skylar Astin) surprise their friend (Justin Chon) on his 21st birthday and then proceed to get into more trouble in one night than members of this film's target audience will encounter in their entire college lives. (Nicole Herrington)
'Upside Down' (PG-13, 1:40) The dreary science-fiction romance starring Kirsten Dunst and Jim Sturgess, sinks under the weight of its pretentious concept about twin planets and its tedious scientific jargon. (Holden)
'Warm Bodies' (PG-13, 1:37) An improbable zombie romance sweetened with appealing performances and buoyed by one of the better cute meets in recent romantic comedy, when the zombie boy, Nicholas Hoult, decides not to eat a live girl, Teresa Palmer. (Dargis)
* 'The We and the I' (No rating, 1:43) In the course of a long, boisterous bus ride through the Bronx on the last day of school, a group of teenagers fight, flirt and reflect on what it is to be young and restless in New York. Their sometimes chaotic interactions are captured by Michel Gondry ("Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind," "Be Kind Rewind") with sympathy and a welcome sense of the latent magic of everyday life. (Scott)
* 'Zero Dark Thirty' (R, 2:37) Kathryn Bigelow's brilliantly directed fictionalized account about the search for Osama bin Laden is a difficult, urgent movie that shows the unspeakable, torture included, and lets us decide if the death of bin Laden was worth the price we paid. Written by Mark Boal, the movie is a seamless weave of truth and drama as well as a wrenchingly sad, soul-shaking story about revenge and its moral costs. See it, fight over it, but don't ignore it. (Dargis)
Film Series
The Films of Stanley Kubrick (Friday through Thursday) With Rodney Ascher's "Room 237," a documentary about the many meanings fans have found in Stanley Kubrick's horror film "The Shining," set to open on next Friday, the IFC Center presents all 13 of Kubrick's features in rotation this week, from his first (and subsequently disowned) fiction film "Fear and Desire" (1953) to his final, uncompleted effort "Eyes Wide Shut" (1999). Steven Spielberg's "A.I. Artificial Intelligence" (2001), based on a Kubrick concept, rounds out the program. 323 Avenue of the Americas, at Third Street, Greenwich Village, (212) 924-7771, ifccenter.com; $13.50. (Dave Kehr)
'Le Pont du Nord' (Friday through Thursday) BAMcinématek screens a new print of Jacques Rivette's enigmatic thriller of 1981, said to have been largely improvised on Paris locations by the director and his stars, Bulle Ogier (as a woman recently released from prison) and her daughter, Pascale Ogier (as a slightly paranoid, self-styled revolutionary). Like Mr. Rivette's debut feature, "Paris Belongs to Us" (1961), "Le Pont du Nord" imagines the city as an immense board game, as the two women follow a suitcase full of clues through a series of chutes and ladders that may or may not be pointing them toward a treasure. BAM Rose Cinemas, 30 Lafayette Avenue, at Ashland Place, Fort Greene, Brooklyn, (718) 636-4100, bam.org; $13. (Kehr)
Andrew Sarris: Expressive Esoterica (Friday through Thursday) Another well-programmed installment of Anthology Film Archives' tribute to the film critic Andrew Sarris, this time featuring nine films saved from obscurity by Sarris's support. Friday's program includes Phil Karlson's chilling late noir "The Brothers Rico" (1957) at 7 p.m. and Joseph H. Lewis's richly atmospheric gothic thriller "My Name Is Julia Ross" (1945) at 9 p.m. Coming up this weekend and next week are Robert Mulligan's "Baby, the Rain Must Fall" (1965), Gerd Oswald's "Fury at Showdown" (1957), Lowell Sherman's "Morning Glory" (1933), John M. Stahl's "Holy Matrimony" (1943), Frank Tashlin's "Bachelor Flat" (1962), Jacques Tourneur's "Wichita" (1955) and Roland West's "Alibi" (1929). All films are in 35-millimeter. 32-34 Second Avenue, at Second Street, East Village, (212) 505-5181, anthologyfilmarchives.org; $10. (Kehr)

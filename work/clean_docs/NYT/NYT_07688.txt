Movies
Ratings and running times are in parentheses; foreign films have English subtitles. Full reviews of all current releases: nytimes.com/movies.
'The Amazing Spider-Man 2' (PG-13, 2:22) The last time Spider-Man swung into sight, it was to fight a lizard man in a lab coat and cozy up to a living doll. The lizard landed in the clink, but the doll, Gwen Stacy (Emma Stone), is back for the good of Peter Parker a.k.a. Spider-Man (Andrew Garfield) and the franchise that again is in the hands of the director Marc Webb. Ms. Stone brightens the movie, which until a late, lamentably foolish turn, generally balances blockbuster bombast with human-scale drama, child-friendly comedy and gushers of tears. Dane DeHaan makes a nice new addition to the mix. (Manohla Dargis)
'American Jesus' (No rating, 1:20) It's not clear what Aram Garriga, a Spanish filmmaker, thinks he is accomplishing with this documentary, which samples out-of-the-mainstream versions of American Christianity, but his examples -- a snake-handling clergyman, a ministry aimed at strippers, and so on -- are already wearyingly familiar from reality TV and such. A few serious thinkers turn up later in the film, but not enough to make it insightful. (Neil Genzlinger)
'Bears' (G, 1:18) Disney's latest animal documentary looks at one hungry year in the life of an Alaska bear and her two cubs. It's not the most dazzling work of nature photography, but the furry stars hold a visual fascination throughout. (Nicolas Rapold)
'Belle' (PG, 1:44) No bodices seem to have been harmed, much less ripped, during the making of "Belle," a period film at once sweeping and intimate, about an 18th-century Englishwoman, Dido Elizabeth Belle (Gugu Mbatha-Raw) who touchingly transcends her historical moment. Melodramatic and grounded in real history, "Belle" was written by Misan Sagay, directed by Amma Asante and is enough of an old-fashioned entertainment that it could have been made in classic Hollywood. Well, except for one little thing that would have probably given the old studio suits apoplexy: its lovely heroine is biracial. The fine supporting cast includes Tom Wilkinson, Sarah Gadon, Miranda Richardson, Penelope Wilton and Emily Watson. (Dargis)
'Brick Mansions' (PG-13, 1:30) This Detroit-set remake of the French action movie "District B13" is fast and loud, as it should be -- with plenty of car chases and parkour -- but it also has a tantalizing hint of class consciousness and a dollop of streetwise wit, mostly thanks to RZA of the Wu-Tang Clan, who plays a drug kingpin with unusual priorities. (A. O. Scott)
'Captain America: The Winter Soldier' (PG-13, 2:16) Given how little creative wiggle room there is in properties like "The Winter Soldier," a sequel to "Captain America: The First Avenger" (2011), it counts as a minor triumph that the directors, the brothers Anthony and Joe Russo, imprint any personality on this new movie, which is less a stand-alone work than a part of an ever-expanding multimedia enterprise. As the World War II veteran turned contemporary superhero, Chris Evans remains a recessive if appealing screen presence. (Dargis)
'Divergent' (PG-13, 2:23) Shailene Woodley stars as Tris Prior in this adaptation of the first book in Veronica Roth's young adult series. It's another dystopian tale that pivots on a gutsy teenage heroine, one living in a postwar Chicago. For all its similarities to "The Hunger Games," "Divergent" isn't as interesting partly because Tris spends a lot of time wondering why her cute male instructor pays attention to her: The story celebrates individuality and breaking out of the little boxes that its authoritarian leaders (i.e., adults) put teenagers in, even as it sticks to the usual gender template. The director, Neal Burger, gives you little to hang on to -- beauty, thrills, a visual style. (Dargis)
'Draft Day' (PG-13, 1:49) This promotional film for the N.F.L. is nothing special, but it is redeemed somewhat by Ivan Reitman's smooth direction and the leathery professionalism of Kevin Costner as a Cleveland Browns general manager facing many tough decisions. (Scott)
'Fading Gigolo' (R, 1:30) John Turturro directed, and stars in, this low-key, low-energy comedy as a former bookstore employee turned prostitute whose pimp is played by none other than a deftly funny Woody Allen. It's a story of New York haves and have-nots that never manages to persuade, despite the engaging supporting likes of Sharon Stone, Sofía Vergara, Vanessa Paradis and especially Liev Schreiber as a hunky Hasidic neighborhood cop. (Dargis)
* 'Fed Up' (PG, 1:39) This very good advocacy documentary (narrated and executive produced by Katie Couric) introduces you to the puzzle of rising obesity rates. From there, it moves into investigative mode, as it explores just who is damaging our health (among them, entities described as Big Sugar and such). Finally, there are answers, surprising and not, which can be reduced to the ugly truth that it isn't always in the best interests of both big business and the government to keep people healthy. (Dargis)
'Finding Vivian Maier' (No rating, 1:23) An exciting electric current of discovery runs through John Maloof and Charlie Siskel's documentary about the street photographer who worked as a care provider and never exhibited her work. Maier is a terrific story -- part Mary Poppins, part Weegee -- and the movie is a solid if finally thin introduction to her. It's also, to state the obvious, a feature-length advertisement for Mr. Maloof's commercial venture as the principal owner of her work and would have been stronger if it had dug into the complexities of what it means when one person assumes ownership of another's art. (Dargis)
The German Doctor' (PG-13, 1:33, in Spanish and German) Set in Patagonia in the 1960s, this quietly jittery art house thriller hinges on a relationship that a young girl, Lilith (Florencia Bado), forms with a stranger (Alex Brendemühl, suave and icy) who turns out to be Josef Mengele. The Argentine writer and director Lucía Puenzo, shooting in wide screen, takes an effective, largely cool approach to her fictionalization of Mengele's time in South America. She overworks some of her more blatant visual motifs, but the blond children at a local German school unsettle the mood nicely. (Dargis)
* 'The Grand Budapest Hotel' (R, 1:39) Wes Anderson's latest film is whimsical, fussy and ingenious -- a Wes Anderson film, in other words -- but it is also a sly and touching engagement with some of the ugliest parts of 20th-century European history. Ralph Fiennes plays a hotel concierge in the fictional Central-European republic of Zubrowka in the fateful year of 1932. The frames are beautifully composed, the production design exquisite and a large cast is dispatched on a complex caper. But all the fun is shadowed by -- and is a brilliant protest against -- some very cruel realities. (Scott)
Poss cut'A Haunted House 2' (R, 1:26) This parody franchise from Marlon Wayans, after only two entries, already displays a sputtering exhaustion. Though he enlists support from the likes of Jaime Pressly, Gabriel Iglesias, the talented Missi Pyle and Cedric the Entertainer (back from the first "Haunted House"), this is Mr. Wayans's show, and his perspiring physicality, wide-eyed mugging and nonstop pop culture references grow exasperating. (Webster)
'Heaven Is for Real' (PG, 1:40) Working with hushed choirs, tasteful beams of light and an ethereal aesthetic, Randall Wallace directs this verdant prairie poem (based on a 2010 book) about a hard-up Nebraska pastor (Greg Kinnear) whose small son surfaces from a ruptured appendix insisting that he saw seraphim and a horse-riding Jesus.
(Jeannette Catsoulis)
* 'Ida' (No rating, 1:20, in Polish) Set in Poland in 1962 and shot in haunting, narrow-screen black-and-white, Pawel Pawlikowski's new film (his first made in his native land and language) is a fable about memory, forgetting and the tragic weight of history. Two women -- a teenage novice about to take her vows as a nun (Agata Trzebuchowska) and her aunt (Agata Kulesza), a cynical Communist official -- search for the truth about what happened to their family during World War II. The two Agatas are unforgettable, and Mr. Pawlikowski tells his relatively simple, small-scale story with such clarity and nuance that it expands before your eyes. (Scott)
'Legends of Oz: Dorothy's Return' (PG, 1:32) Dorothy is called back to topple a dictatorial jester in this animated adaptation of a spinoff by Roger Stanton Baum, L. Frank Baum's great-grandson. Sadly for Oz fans, it's generally second-rate, but there are some fine details to be found in its uneven visuals and lively voice work by Martin Short and Megan Hilty. (Rapold)
'Locke' (R, 1:25) Steven Knight's movie about a man in lockdown -- emotionally, psychologically, existentially -- asks you to spend 85 minutes in intimate quarters with Tom Hardy, who's mostly seen in medium close-up behind a steering wheel. It's a story about a man on the verge of making a life-changing decision and, like a lot of road movies, the journey is the destination. Moment by moment, with a twitch, a shudder, a look, Mr. Hardy movingly draws you in, turning a stranger's face into a life. (Dargis)
* 'The Lunchbox' (PG, 1:44, in Hindi) Ritesh Batra's charming debut, set in Mumbai, refreshes an old and sturdy conceit (perhaps most familiar from "You've Got Mail"). Two lonely souls -- a neglected young wife (Nimrat Kaur) and a middle-aged, widowed office worker (Irrfan Khan) -- pursue an anonymous correspondence that begins when a home-cooked lunch is delivered to the wrong place. (Scott)
'The M Word' (R, 2:00) As cavalier with structure and as warmly humane as ever, Henry Jaglom wades into the battle between women and their hormones -- and between money and creativity -- at a limping television station in Los Angeles. (Catsoulis)
* 'Manakamana' (No rating, 1:58, in Nepali and English) Stephanie Spray and Pacho Velez's transporting ethnographic film, set in a green sliver of Nepal, consists of 11 uncut shots, most taken in a closed cable car. It's a fascinating trip filled with men, women, children and the occasional animal who make for as gripping viewing as the landscape unfolding behind them. For 10 or so minutes at a time, each floats hundreds of feet above a lush forest, traveling to and from the Hindu temple that gives this film its rhythmic title. By focusing on such a narrow slice of life, Ms. Spray and Mr. Velez have ceded any totalizing claim on the truth and instead settled for a perfect incompleteness. Essential viewing. (Dargis)
'Moms' Night Out' (PG, 1:38) This Christian-themed comedy, trying desperately to prove that adherents can be wild and crazy, mistakes shrillness for humor. Sarah Drew plays a young mother who is outmatched by the job of being a parent, Sean Astin is her husband, and when he tries to take the kids for an evening so she can go out with her gal pals, mayhem ensues. None of it is funny, but the film does make a pretty good case that Christians make lousy parents. (Genzlinger)
'Muppets Most Wanted' (PG, 1:47) In this follow-up to the 2011 Muppet movie, Kermit lands in a gulag while an evil frog who bears a striking resemblance to him takes his place on an international Muppet tour. The charm of the earlier film is replaced with plotting complexities, and the movie has more grown-up jokes than its predecessor. It's sometimes funny but rarely lovable. (Genzlinger)
'Noah' (PG-13, 2:18) Darren Aronofsky's version of the familiar Sunday school story is a scary, brooding environmental allegory, in which Noah (Russell Crowe) takes his divine instructions as a warrant for fanatical, almost genocidal behavior. Noah, assisted by stone giants, is pitted against Tubal-Cain (Ray Winstone), who wants to save corrupt humanity from its creator's wrath. The movie is clumsy and not always coherent, but at its mad best it is a powerful, sincere attempt to find modern meanings in the Old Testament. (Scott)
'Oculus' (R, 1:44) This derivative but efficient chiller that cribs from "Solaris," "The Shining" and "The Amityville Horror" also shows glimmers of imagination. The elegant wide-screen, however, can't totally obscure stiff acting. (Ben Kenigsberg)
'Only Lovers Left Alive' (R, 2:03) Tilda Swinton and Tom Hiddleston are the coolest vampires ever in Jim Jarmusch's latest movie, a seductive tribute to its own aesthetic preoccupations. (Scott)
'The Other Woman' (PG-13, 1:49) Cameron Diaz has her hands full with this female revenge comedy, a shrill, lazy, clumsily assembled rip-off of "The First Wives Club" that could crush any actor forced to execute its leaden slapstick gags and mouth its crude humorless dialogue.
(Stephen Holden)
'Palo Alto' (R, 1:38) This feature directing debut from Gia Coppola (her grandfather is Francis Ford Coppola), working from a collection of James Franco's short stories about teenagers in Northern California, captures that exquisitely tender, moving moment between fragile, self-interested youth and more outwardly aware adulthood. (Dargis)
* 'Particle Fever' (No rating, 1:39) This documentary, directed by Mark Levinson, chronicles the work of theoretical and experimental physicists involved with the Large Hadron Collider, an enormous gizmo near Geneva that in 2012 isolated the elusive Higgs boson (a subatomic morsel believed to hold the key to understanding the universe). If that sentence is clear to you, you should buy a ticket now, but even if the intricacies of physics lie beyond your grasp, the grandeur of the enterprise and the humanity of the scientists themselves make for mind-blowing viewing. (Scott)
'The Quiet Ones' (PG-13, 1:38) In this latest horror movie about weird doings in the 1970s, Jared Harris stars as an Oxford University professor who believes the supernatural occurrences in a patient's bleak, lonely life have been created by her mind and is using every bad idea he has -- including curing her through sleep deprivation -- to prove it. As in other horror tales with doctors and scientists, the darkness emerges during the struggle between an ostensibly rational mind and the creatures that are manifestations of irrationality and, by their very existence, emblems of religious faith. Too bad it isn't scary. (Dargis)
* 'The Railway Man' (R, 1:56) Is there a place today for a solid, old-fashioned movie about war, remembrance and reconciliation? This unabashedly stodgy, high-minded film in the David Lean tradition (but minus the grandeur) stars Colin Firth, giving a devastating understated portrayal of Eric Lomax, a British Army officer who was captured and tortured by the Japanese during World War II. (Holden)
'Rio 2' (G, 1:41) Rejoining the pair of blue macaws (still voiced by Jesse Eisenberg and Anne Hathaway) from his previous feature, Carlos Saldanha's frantic musical smothers its pops of avant-garde flavor in the retro rhythms of comic formula and the relentless beats of choreographed stimulation. (Catsoulis)'Stage Fright' (R, 1:28) Early on, this Canadian musical comedy offers a glimpse of exactly the sort of demented, toe-tapping sensibility you'd expect from a tuneful summer camp farce -- in which a serial killer is on the loose. The film is inspired enough to draw attention to ways that it doesn't realize its potential. (Kenigsberg)
'Transcendence' (PG-13, 1:59) Johnny Depp is weirdly perfect as an artificial-intelligence genius who becomes the monster of his own creation, or so it seems after his consciousness is uploaded onto the Internet. Directed by Wally Pfister, a cinematographer making his feature directing debut, this inelegant, no doubt implausible (maybe not) science-fiction film is a dark, lurchingly entertaining pastiche of age-old worries, future-shock jolts, hot-button topics and old-fashioned genre thrills. (Dargis)
* 'Under the Skin' (R, 1:48) In this somber sci-fi horror movie, Scarlett Johansson plays a predatory alien who assumes a human identity and lures men to their deaths. Jonathan Glazer's absorbing movie leaves you reflecting on the notion that every being in the universe is an alien in disguise. (Holden)
'Walking With the Enemy' (No rating, 2:04) This fictionalized account is based on the real-life deeds of Pinchas Rosenbaum, a Hungarian rabbi's son who was able to save Jews from the Nazis and their collaborators by disguising himself in enemy garb. The movie shows once again that the imperatives of melodrama and the indifferent slaughter of the Holocaust make for an uneasy mix. (Kenigsberg)
* 'Le Week-End' (R, 1:33) Nick (Jim Broadbent) and Meg (Lindsay Duncan), a left-leaning, academic British baby boomer couple, arrive in Paris to celebrate their 30th anniversary. But are we witnessing the celebration of a long marriage or its sudden unraveling? The writer Hanif Kureishi and the director Roger Michell (whose previous collaborations include "The Mother" and "The Buddha of Suburbia") confect what is on the surface a bittersweet comedy of love in maturity. But there are strong, angry passions under the surface (particularly as far as Meg is concerned) and an eruption of sharp social satire when Jeff Goldblum shows up playing an old graduate school chum of Nick's who has matured into a happy and prosperous sellout. (Scott)
Young & Beautiful' (No rating, 1:35) François Ozon's coming-of-age story, in which an upper-middle-class French high school student (Marine Vacth) takes up prostitution as an extracurricular activity, is coy and prurient rather than illuminating, and oddly dull even when it tries to be provocative. (Scott) Film Series
Cool Worlds: The Animation of Ralph Bakshi (through Tuesday) "Animals having sex isn't pornographic." This argument wasn't enough to save Ralph Bakshi's "Fritz the Cat" from becoming the first X-rated animated feature. And he was just getting started. Actually, Mr. Bakshi got his start working on Saturday-morning TV fare like "The Deputy Dawg Show" and "Spider-Man," but 1972's "Fritz" out-scandalized its source underground comics of the same name by R. Crumb -- no mean feat. (It screens at various times on May 17.) Multiple screenings at various times, BAM Rose Cinemas, 30 Lafayette Avenue, at Ashland Place, Fort Greene, Brooklyn, 718-636-4100, bam.org; $13. (Eric GrodeFassbinder: Romantic Anarchist (Part 1) (Friday through June 1) The German enfant terrible Rainer Werner Fassbinder receives his most thorough New York retrospective since 1997 -- and it's big enough to be broken up into two halves. In a mere 13 years, Fassbinder produced an oeuvre of almost 40 films, drawing on a ferociously dedicated corps of actors as he hopscotched across genres. (It helped that he typically shot his films in less than three weeks.)Highlights in Part 1 include off-kilter adaptations of Ibsen ("Nora Helmer," screening Wednesday at 3 p.m. and Thursday at 4:45 and 9 p.m.); American gangster movies ("Gods of the Plague," Friday at 1 and 5 p.m. and Saturday at 7 p.m.); and the 212-minute "World on a Wire" (May 25 and 26), which served as a head-spinning template for the likes of "The Matrix" and "Blade Runner." The series will also feature Douglas Sirk's "All That Heaven Allows," which influenced Fassbinder's "Ali: Fear Eats the Soul," and Todd Haynes's "Far From Heaven," which drew from both films. Elinor Bunin Munroe Film Center, Lincoln Center, 144 West 65th Street, Manhattan, 212-875-5601, filmlinc.com; $13. (Grode)
From Mae West to Punk: The Bowery on Film (Friday through Monday) Is it really that far a jump from Mae West's polished provocations to the punk-rock sounds that pumped out of CBGB? Maybe Bugs Bunny, who mentioned his Lower East Side origins in "A Hare Grows in Manhattan," would be a better place to start. That cartoon will be presented along with nearly 20 other films and shorts chronicling what has been called New York City's oldest thoroughfare. Raoul Walsh hired prostitutes and homeless people for his first feature film, 1915's "Regeneration" (screening Friday at 7:15 p.m.), while Lionel Rogosin paid unblinking tribute to its ravaged denizens in the 1956 semi-documentary "On the Bowery" (Saturday at 7:30 p.m. and Monday at 7 p.m.). Anthology Film Archives, 32-34 Second Avenue, at Second Street, East Village, 212-505-5181, anthologyfilmarchives.org; $10. (Grode)
John Hubley: Two Centennial Tributes (Tuesday and May 27) At Film Forum. See photo highlight. On the basis of sheer minute-by-minute joy, these cartoons by the masterly John Hubley -- a former Disney animator who set up the independent studio Storyboard in 1953 after being blacklisted -- are hard to beat. Whether it's an imaginative asterisk conjuring all sorts of surreal wonderment in "Adventure of an *" or his sons' surreptitiously recorded bedtime adventure in the Academy Award-winning "Moonbird," these tales by Hubley and his wife, Faith, shimmer and delight. Hubley's effervescent light touch rivaled that of the jazz greats he used for his soundtracks, including Oscar Peterson, Benny Carter and Dizzy Gillespie (who appears in "A Date With Dizzy," a live-action short that incorporates Hubley animations into an irresistibly subversive commentary on commercialization). And, yes, Hubley's best-known creation -- the myopic Mr. Magoo -- will make an appearance on both programs. Tuesday at 7:40 p.m., May 27 at 6:30 p.m., Film Forum, 209 West Houston Street, west of Avenue of the Americas, South Village, 212-727-8110, filmforum.org; $13. (Grode)
Mizoguchi (through June 8) We have only seven of the 123 plays that Sophocles wrote and 30 of the 85 films that Kenji Mizoguchi directed. Mizoguchi may have the higher percentage of surviving pieces, but he also worked his rigorous, haunting magic about 2,300 years after Sophocles did -- and losing 55 films even half as good as Mizoguchi's "Sansho the Bailiff" and "Ugetsu" is heartbreaking. Museum of the Moving Image's essential retrospective will feature those, as well as several films that have rarely been seen in the United States, including "Song of Home," his earliest existing film, and "Oyuki the Virgin," based on a Guy de Maupassant story about two "fallen women" (a pet subject for Mizoguchi) who teach the higher classes a few things about morality. 35th Avenue at 37th Street, Astoria, Queens, 718-784-0077, movingimage.us; $12. (Eric Grode)
Punk Rock Girls (through June 1) BAMcinématek's 12-film survey of women who know how to make some noise continues after kicking off on Wednesday with Lukas Moodysson's latest, "We Are the Best!" Many of the directors of the other films in this series surrounded their stars -- including Diane Lane ("Ladies and Gentlemen, the Fabulous Stains," on May 31), Rosario Dawson (the surprisingly subversive "Josie and the Pussycats," on May 29) and Rosanna Arquette ("Desperately Seeking Susan," on June 1) -- with real-life punk rockers like Siouxsie Sioux, Richard Hell and the Slits. Slava Tsukerman, who directed the 1982 cult classic "Liquid Sky," will introduce that film on May 30. He also co-wrote its ominous, synth-heavy score, considered by many to be the jumping-off point for electroclash music. BAM Rose Cinemas, 30 Lafayette Avenue, at Ashland Place, Fort Greene, Brooklyn, 718-636-4100, bam.org; $13. (Grode)
Semper, Roi, Semper: Amiri Baraka (1934-2014) (through Sunday) Is there anyone Amiri Baraka hadn't managed to infuriate by the time he died in January? Over the course of a varied career, Baraka -- previously LeRoi Jones -- made enough stylistic, philosophical and political pivots to create his own virtual countercultural anthology. The five programs that are part of this series touch on a range of Baraka moments: the 1967 film version of "Dutchman," the scorching 1964 play that put him, as Jones, on the map; a xylophone-toting Baraka's surreal street invocation in "1 PM" (Friday and Sunday), the 1972 documentary by Jean-Luc Godard, Richard Leacock and D.A. Pennebaker; and "Somebody Blew Up America," the poem about the Sept. 11 attacks that landed him in hot water during his term as New Jersey poet laureate (Friday). Anthology Film Archives, 32-34 Second Avenue, at Second Street, East Village, 212-505-5181, anthologyfilmarchives.org; $10. (Grode)

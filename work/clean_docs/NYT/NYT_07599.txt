'ODD DAY RAIN'
If you think children spend too much time with computers today, imagine their lives in 200 years.
That's exactly what Janine Nina Trevens and Deirdre Broderick have done in "Odd Day Rain," a new musical from Tada! Youth Theater. A science-fiction story that unfolds in what looks like a barren, post-apocalyptic landscape, the show represents a striking departure for this ensemble of young actors (ages 10 to 17), both in its futuristic setting and in its slashing, propulsive rock score. (Ms. Broderick wrote the music and lyrics; both authors collaborated on the book.) While the production, directed by Ms. Trevens, does offer a happy ending, its unsettling portrait of a distant generation's digital dependence can't help evoking our own.
"Odd Day Rain," titled after the unusual weather in 2211 -- precipitation falls for just one hour every other day -- begins with a group of ragtag, apparently homeless children, scrounging outdoors. Some talk of the time "before the accident," and one, Aurora (Christina Franklin and Ariana Sepulveda alternate in the role), says she believes that she can get inside "the ruin" and prove that a girl still lives there.
That girl, Claire (Allie Korbey, shown above center with other cast members, alternates with Brielle Raddi), exists in a cell-like chamber housing an all-powerful artificial intelligence. This -- HAL, please meet your digital descendant -- is Computer (Meaghan Turner, whose face appears in a huge video projection representing the machine's monitor). Addressing Claire in the tones of mellifluous menace that HAL made famous in Stanley Kubrick's film "2001: A Space Odyssey," Computer governs her life, fulfilling her every need but also holding her prisoner. Claire attends school and meets friends via the Internet; her home has no door.
Whether Aurora's real world will breach Claire's virtual one during the narrow window of opportunity -- Computer shuts down only during the rain -- propels the musical's plot, which wisely doesn't condemn technology entirely. Some theatergoers, though, will be frustrated by the mysteries it leaves unsolved: What was "the accident"? How did Aurora and her band escape? And whatever happened to all these kids' parents?
I'd really like to know. But "Odd Day Rain" will provoke plenty of thought with another question, one not easily answered: How much virtual interaction can human society embrace before it ceases to be human?

'Archaeology Zone: Discovering Treasures From Playgrounds to Palaces' (Friday, Sunday through Tuesday, and Thursday) Children will step into the shoes of an explorer like Indiana Jones in this permanent exhibition at the Jewish Museum, but the adventures will be purely scholarly. Still, there is plenty of excitement in analyzing artifacts like a jar handle, a clay jug and a bangle and figuring out the purpose behind ancient pieces like a Greek helmet and a bull-shaped vessel. This interactive show also includes a re-created room from the Ottoman period (about 1900), where young archaeologists can dress in costume.

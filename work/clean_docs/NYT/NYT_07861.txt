Theaters are in Manhattan unless otherwise noted. Full reviews of current productions, additional listings, showtimes and ticket information are at nytimes.com/theater. Previews and Openings
'China Doll' (in previews; opens on Dec. 4) Some dolls talk. David Mamet doesn't. After the early closure of "The Anarchist" and a couple of modest revivals, Mr. Mamet has returned to Broadway with a new play, starring Al Pacino and Christopher Denham, directed by Pam MacKinnon. Mr. Pacino reportedly plays a lion of some industry or other, now on the verge of retirement, but few other details have emerged. Gerald Schoenfeld Theater, 236 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, chinadollbroadway.com. (Alexis Soloski)
'The Color Purple' (in previews; opens on Dec. 10) Is purple John Doyle's color? The celebrated director typically works in more modest, minimalist tones, with maximal impact. Following a successful run in London, he brings this stripped-down revival of the musical by Marsha Norman, Brenda Russell, Allee Willis and Stephen Bray, based on the Alice Walker novel, to Broadway. The breakout star Cynthia Erivo reprises her role as Celie. Jennifer Hudson and Danielle Brooks also star. Bernard B. Jacobs Theater, 242 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, colorpurple.com. (Soloski)
'Fiddler on the Roof' (previews start on Friday; opens on Dec. 20) Sunrise, sunset, swiftly flow the days and suddenly it's time for a new revival of this shtetl-set musical by Joseph Stein, Sheldon Harnick and Jerry Bock. Danny Burstein and Jessica Hecht star as Tevye and Golde. Bartlett Sher, fresh from the Siam of "The King and I" directs. Broadway Theater, 1681 Broadway at 53rd Street, 212-239-6200, fiddlermusical.com. (Soloski)
'Gigantic' (in previews; opens on Dec. 3) The Vineyard Theater steps on to the scales with this new musical set at Camp Overton, a weight loss retreat for teens. The writers Randy Blair and Tim Drucker and the composer Matthew roi Berger take a comic look at adolescent angst and low-fat meals. Scott Schwartz directs the fitness regime. Acorn Theater at Theater Row, 410 West 42nd Street, Clinton, 212-239-6200, vineyardtheatre.org. (Soloski)
'Important Hats of the Twentieth Century' (in previews; opens on Monday) The writer Nick Jones is always in fashion. A writer and producer on "Orange Is the New Black," he still manages an Off Broadway play nearly every year. Set in 1930s New York, Carson Elrod stars as a couturier whose chief rival (Matt Saldivar) starts designing futuristic items like sweatshirts and skater pants. Moritz von Stuelpnagel directs the ensemble. The Studio at Stage II, New York City Center, 131 West 55th Street, 212-581-1212, nycitycenter.org. (Soloski)
'Invisible Thread' (in previews; opens on Dec. 2) When the playwrights and composers Matt Gould and Griffin Matthews were in their 20s, they both did stints as aid workers in Africa, experiences that have now inspired a musical. In this Second Stage production, Mr. Matthews plays a version of himself, confronting ideas about sexuality and altruism. Diane Paulus directs the philanthropic fare. Second Stage Theater, 305 West 43rd Street, 212-246-4422, 2st.com. (Soloski)
'Lazarus' (in previews; opens on Dec. 7) Call it the rock god who landed Off Broadway. David Bowie supplies the songs and some of the story for this new music theater piece inspired by the 1963 Walter Tevis novel, "The Man Who Fell to Earth," about an extraterrestrial stranded in America. (Mr. Bowie famously starred in the 1976 film adaptation.) Ivo van Hove directs a script by Enda Walsh and Mr. Bowie, with Michael C. Hall as the space invader and Cristin Milioti as his earthly assistant. New York Theater Workshop, 79 East Fourth Street, East Village, 212-279-4200, nytw.org. (Soloski)
'Marjorie Prime' (previews start on Friday; opens on Dec. 14) In Jordan Harrison's futuristic drama, Lois Smith stars as an octogenarian who is gifted an artificial-intelligence version of her deceased husband. For Playwrights Horizons, Anne Kauffman directs a play that examines memory, loss and connection -- human and otherwise. Noah Bean, Lisa Emery and Stephen Root co-star. 416 West 42nd Street, 212-279-4200, playwrightshorizons.org. (Soloski)
'New York Animals' (in previews; opens on Nov. 29) Bedlam Theater made its name with its stripped-down, amped-up versions of the classics, with too few actors somehow managing too many roles. This time Bedlam is taking on a new play by Steven Sater, though still with its signature mini cast. Mr. Sater's script follows 21 New Yorkers over the course of a rainy afternoon, with new songs by Burt Bacharach. New Ohio Theater, 154 Christopher Street, West Village, 888-596-1027, theatrebedlam.org. (Soloski)
'Night Is a Room' (in previews; opens on Sunday) The lyrical playwright Naomi Wallace dries off from her last Signature Theater outing, "The Liquid Plain," with this contemporary drama. Dagmara Dominczyk and Bill Heck star as a married couple whose nuptial bliss is threatened by Ann Dowd's Doré. The director Bill Rauch calculates the angles of this love triangle. Pershing Square Signature Center, Alice Griffin Jewel Box Theater, 480 West 42nd Street, 212-244-7529, signaturetheatre.org. (Soloski)
'Nora' (in previews; opens on Sunday) Nora will slam the door anew in Ingmar Bergman's adaptation of Henrik Ibsen's classic drama, directed by Austin Pendleton. A story of a woman who finds herself a stranger in her own marriage, its cast includes George Morfogen, Jean Lichty, Larry Bull, Andrea Cirie and Todd Gearhart. Cherry Lane, 38 Commerce Street, West Village, 866-811-4111, cherrylanetheatre.org. (Soloski)
'Once Upon a Mattress' (previews start on Monday; opens on Dec. 10) This Transport Group production at Abrons Arts Center presents a familiar musical with an unusual cast. Jack Cummings III directs the fractured fairy tale starring Jackie Hoffman as the intrepid princess Winnifred and John Epperson (often known as Lipsynka) as a mean queen. 466 Grand Street, at Pitt Street, Lower East Side, 866-811-4111, abronsartscenter.org. (Soloski)
'School of Rock: The Musical' (in previews; opens on Dec. 6) A bungling substitute teacher gives a musical education and gets a sentimental one in this new musical from Andrew Lloyd Webber, with lyrics by Glenn Slater and a book by the "Downton Abbey" creator Julian Fellowes. Based on the 2003 Jack Black flick, it stars Alex Brightman as the shredding educator with Sierra Boggess as the strait-laced principal. Winter Garden Theater, 1634 Broadway, between 50th and 51st Streets, schoolofrockthemusical.com. (Soloski)
'These Paper Bullets!' (previews start on Friday; opens on Dec. 15) Will it be much ado about something when the playwright Rolin Jones ("The Intelligent Design of Jenny Chow") resets Shakespeare's comedy in London's swinging '60s? At the Atlantic Theater, Billie Joe Armstrong supplies the mod songs, Jackson Gay directs the groovy action and Justin Kirk and Nicole Parker are the updated Ben and Bea. Linda Gross Theater, 336 West 20th Street, 866-811-4111, atlantictheater.org. (Soloski)
Broadway
'Allegiance' A singing history lesson about a dark chapter in American history, the internment of Japanese-Americans during World War II. Jay Kuo's score is unfortunately derivative, and the book feels overburdened by the need to both entertain and inform. George Takei ("Star Trek," duh!) and the still plush-voiced Lea Salonga star (2:20). Longacre Theater, 220 West 48th Street, allegiancemusical.com, 212-239-6200. (Charles Isherwood)
* 'An American in Paris' The ballet luminary Christopher Wheeldon makes a triumphant debut as a Broadway director with this rhapsodic stage adaptation of a classic musical with a heavenly Gershwin score. The ballet dancers Robert Fairchild and Leanne Cope, as the semi-star-crossed lovers, are radiant, and Max von Essen sings like a dream as an heir who aspires to a nightclub career. Pure joy (2:30). The Palace Theater. 1564 Broadway, at 47th Street, 877-250-2929, ticketmaster.com. (Isherwood)
'Dames at Sea' This revival of the 1966 spoof of backstage movie musicals of the 1930s has a wee, winning cast that could fit in a dinghy, and scads of energetic choreography by Randy Skinner, who also directs. Still, Broadway has been on a nostalgia trip for so long that this small-scaled show feels just a bit, well, at sea on Broadway (2:05). Helen Hayes Theater, 240 West 44th Street, 212-239-6200, damesatseabroadway.com. (Isherwood)
'Finding Neverland' This musical adaptation of the 2004 biopic about J.M. Barrie, the creator of "Peter Pan," heightens the film's tidy psychologizing and life-affirming messages by thickening their syrup and corn quotients. The show brings to mind those supersize sodas sold in movie theaters. It's mostly empty calories. Diane Paulus directs a cast led by Matthew Morrison (2:30). Lunt-Fontanne Theater, 205 West 46th Street, 877-250-2929, ticketmaster.com. (Ben Brantley)
* 'Fool for Love' Love as a battlefield on which nobody wins has seldom been mapped as thrillingly as it is in Daniel Aukin's definitive revival of Sam Shepard's bruising 1983 drama. Playing May and Eddie, a couple locked in an eternal apache dance, Nina Arianda and Sam Rockwell exude the sort of chemistry of which nuclear meltdowns are made (1:15). Samuel J. Friedman Theater, 261 West 47th Street, 212-239-6200, foolforlovebroadway.com. (Brantley)
* 'Fun Home' This extraordinary musical memory play about a girl and her father, adapted by Jeanine Tesori (music) and Lisa Kron (book and lyrics) from Alison Bechdel's graphic novel, occupies that mysterious place where we all grew up -- the shifting landscape governed by those contradictory creatures, our parents. Sam Gold directs a nigh-flawless cast in a show that brings fresh oxygen to Broadway (1:40). Circle in the Square Theater, 1633 Broadway, at 50th Street, funhomebroadway.com, 212-239-6200. (Brantley)
* 'The Gin Game' A superlative James Earl Jones and Cicely Tyson headline the director Leonard Foglia's excellent revival of D.L. Coburn's durable 1976 comedy about two residents of a retirement home who bond -- and bicker -- over the card table (2:00). Golden Theater, 252 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, thegingamebroadway.com. (Isherwood)
* 'Hamilton' Yes, it really is that good. Lin-Manuel Miranda's rap-driven portrait of the rise and fall of Alexander Hamilton, directed by Thomas Kail, makes us feel the unstoppable, urgent rhythm of a nation being born. A show that changes the language of the American musical, while offering resounding evidence that this beleaguered genre is not only surviving but thriving (2:45). Richard Rodgers Theater, 226 West 46th Street, 877-250-2929, hamiltonbroadway.com. (Brantley)
* 'Hand to God' Robert Askins's black comedy, about a mild Christian boy whose hand puppet may (or may not) be possessed by the Devil, is as outlandishly funny as it is seriously spooky. Steven Boyer gives a bravura performance as the troubled teenager with the evil imp on his arm, and Geneva Carr is moving as his addled, newly widowed mother. The play, previously seen in two Off Broadway runs, has grown even sharper, and more disturbing, in its move uptown to Broadway (2:00). Booth Theater, 222 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, handtogodbroadway.com. (Isherwood)
'The Illusionists: Live on Broadway' This elaborate magic show returns from its run last year at the Marquis Theater with three new members on its roster of seven tricksters. There are laser beams, digital video screens and more. Think of it as a magic show on steroids (2:00). Neil Simon Theater, 250 West 52nd Street, 800-745-3000, theillusionistslive.com. (Isherwood)
* 'The King and I' Bartlett Sher's resplendent revival of this Rodgers and Hammerstein classic, set in the royal court of 19th-century Siam, is both panoramic and personal, balancing epic sweep with intimate sensibility, lavish set pieces with exquisitely sung soliloquies. Kelli O'Hara's Anna Leonowens is something wonderful indeed (2:50). Vivian Beaumont Theater, Lincoln Center, 212-239-6200, lct.org. (Brantley)
* 'King Charles III' Mike Bartlett's flat-out brilliant portrait of a monarchy in crisis -- set in and around Buckingham Palace in a highly foreseeable future -- takes all manner of audacious license, poetic and otherwise, treating its contemporary subject (the House of Windsor) in the style of Shakespeare's history plays. His presumption pays off big-time. Tim Pigott-Smith is splendid as the wavering title character (2:30). Music Box Theater, 239 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, kingcharlesiiibroadway.com. (Brantley)
'Misery' Though based on one of Stephen King's most terrifying novels, this stage adaptation (by William Goldman) is unlikely to leave anyone cold with terror. Starring a vacant Bruce Willis in his Broadway debut and a hard-working Laurie Metcalf, this story of a best-selling author held captive by a psychopathic fan sustains a steady, drowsy room temperature throughout (1:30). Broadhurst Theater, 235 West 44th Street, 212-239-6200, miserybroadway.com. (Brantley)
'Old Times' Douglas Hodge's flashy revival of Harold Pinter's drama of memory and power games within a shifting triangle may irritate Pinter purists, with its cosmic staging. (It can feel like "Old Times: Armageddon.") But its skillful, charismatic cast -- Eve Best, Kelly Reilly and Clive Owen -- entertainingly makes it clear as to who's on top at any given moment (1:05). American Airlines Theater, 227 West 42nd Street, 212-719-1300, roundabouttheatre.org. (Brantley)
'On Your Feet!' This latest jukebox musical to plug in to Broadway's power strip tells the story of Gloria and Emilio Estefan's rise to pop-chart success with crowd-pleasing flair and plenty of itchy-feet-making dance music. The vibrant Ana Villafañe and Josh Segarra star as the Cuban-born Estefans (2:20). Marquis Theater, 1535 Broadway, at 45th Street, Manhattan, 877-250-2929, onyourfeetmusical.com. (Isherwood)
'Something Rotten!' This rambunctious show, which weds the Elizabethan theater and the brassy Broadway musical, dances dangerously on the line between tireless and tedious. But the large cast, which includes Brian D'Arcy James and Christian Borle, remains as wired as Adderall-popping sophomores during exam week. Casey Nicholaw directed this exhausting frolic from Wayne and Karey Kirkpatrick, and John O'Farrell (2:20). St. James Theater, 246 West 44th Street, 212-239-6200, rottenbroadway.com. (Brantley)
* 'Spring Awakening' One of the great Broadway musicals of the past decade is born anew with this inventive revival from the Deaf West Theater company, directed with remarkable finesse by Michael Arden. A vibrant cast of both deaf and hearing actors share the roles in this inspiriting production of the show about adolescent angst in 19th-century Germany, with a fluid book by Steven Sater and a gorgeous score by Duncan Sheik (2:20). Brooks Atkinson Theater, 256 West 47th Street, 877-250-2929, springawakeningthemusical.com. (Isherwood)
'Sylvia' Matthew Broderick plays an emotionally adrift middle-aged man who finds solace -- and something close to love -- in the company of a stray mutt he brings home from the park one day. Annaleigh Ashford bounds around with ceaseless energy as the titular dog, and Julie White gives fine support as the exasperated wife, but A.R. Gurney's comedy ultimately has all the heft of a teacup poodle (2:05). Cort Theater, 138 West 48th Street, 212-239-6200, sylviabroadway.com. (Isherwood)
'Thérèse Raquin' Keira Knightley plays the title role of an adulterous wife with dedicated intensity and joylessness in Helen Edmundson's adaptation of Émile Zola's novel. Directed by Evan Cabnet, the show is so relentlessly bleak that you realize from the get-go that happiness in not in the cards, for either the doomed heroine or this production's audience (2:30). Studio 54, 254 West 54th Street, 212-719-1300, roundabouttheatre.org. (Brantley)
* 'A View From the Bridge' This is what Greek tragedy must once have felt like. Ivo van Hove's magnificent reconception of Arthur Miller's drama about a blue-collar Brooklyn family takes you into extreme emotional territory that you seldom dare visit in real life. As the doomed longshoreman Eddie Carbone, Mark Strong gives the most powerful performance you're likely to see this year (1:55). Lyceum Theater, 149 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, lct.org. (Brantley)
Off Broadway
'Abyss' In the German playwright Maria Milisavljevic's poetic mystery play about a missing woman, any fixed sense of self -- in terms of nationality, personal history and relationships familial and sexual -- is under siege. Directed by Maria Mileaf, featuring an ardent young three-member cast, "Abyss" make strike impatient theatergoers as excessively arty. But it is also genuinely artful (1:40). Theaterlab, 357 West 36th Street, 866-811-4111, playco.org. (Brantley)
'The Bachelors' An unlikely play about three adult men who refuse to leave Fraternity Row, Caroline V. McGraw's script keeps asking its characters to behave in ways too absurd for comedy and not funny enough for farce. Still, under Portia Krieger's direction, the fine actors are entertaining to watch and fearless in the amount of stage booze they consume (1:30). Rattlestick Playwrights Theater, 224 Waverly Place, West Village, 866-811-4111, lesseramerica.com. (Soloski)
'Before Your Very Eyes' This ingenious but ultimately unsurprising offering from the media-mixing arts collective Gob Squad is a downer with an upper of a cast. It takes a lively and talented group of young people, puts them in a petri dish of a room, and lets the audience observe as they grow old via the theatrical equivalent of time-lapse photography (1:10). Public Theater, 425 Lafayette Street at Astor Place, East Village, 212-967-7555, publictheater.org. (Brantley)
'Clever Little Lies' Marlo Thomas plays the mother of a straying young husband in Joe DiPietro's comfort-food comedy-drama, which feels like a throwback to Neil Simon's early work from the 1960s (1:30). Westside Theater Upstairs, 407 West 43rd Street, cleverlittlelies.com, 212-239-6200. (Isherwood)
* 'Colin Quinn: The New York Story' Mr. Quinn's joke-dense monologue is a lovely nostalgic lament for a New York gone by (1:15). Cherry Lane Theater, 28 Commerce Street, West Village, 866-811-4111, colinquinnthenewyorkstory.com. (Jason Zinoman)
'Dada Woof Papa Hot' There's something off-puttingly insular about the world of privilege depicted in Peter Parnell's comedy-drama about the new frontier of gay marriage and parenthood. But for the most part, the play's themes -- how gay men's approaches to sex can radically differ; whether fractured relationships can ever be repaired; and how children can alter the sexual and emotional dynamics between any couple -- are handled with nuance and insight by an impeccable cast (1:35). Mitzi E. Newhouse Theater, 150 West 65th Street, 212-239-6200, lct.org. (Isherwood)
'Daddy Long Legs' This sweet, beautifully sung and only occasionally unsettling musical adaptation of Jean Webster's 1912 novel is predicated on the lengthy correspondence between a pert orphan and her anonymous benefactor. Even if the relationship between the adorable Jerusha and the debonair Jervis reads as at least a little creepy, their voices twine charmingly (2:15). Davenport Theater, 354 West 45th Street, 212-239-6200, daddylonglegsmusical.com. (Soloski)
'Dear Elizabeth' The lifelong friendship of the poets Elizabeth Bishop and Robert Lowell is dramatized (albeit minimally) in this moving epistolary play by Sarah Ruhl. Rotating casts of performers take the two roles; this week stars Cherry Jones and David Aaron Baker (through Saturday); Ellen McLaughlin and Rinde Eckert (Monday through Nov. 28); and Mia Katigbak and Harris Yulin (Nov. 30 through Dec. 5). McGinn/ Cazale Theater, 2162 Broadway at 76th Street, Upper West Side, 212-765-1706, wptheater.org. (Isherwood)
* 'Eclipsed' The Oscar winner Lupita Nyong'o leads a superb cast in Danai Gurira's intimate, riveting drama about the sexual enslavement of women during the civil wars in Liberia (2:15). Public Theater, 425 Lafayette Street, at Astor Place, East Village, 212-967-7555, publictheater.org. (Isherwood)
* 'The Flick' Annie Baker's Pulitzer Prize-winning play about the beauty and sadness in the lives of three workers in a run-down movie theater has been remounted under the deeply focused direction of Sam Gold. Moving, funny, unforgettable, but definitely a polarizing night at the theater, thanks to Ms. Baker's quietly observational pacing (3:10). Barrow Street Theater, 27 Barrow Street, at Seventh Avenue South, West Village, 212-868-4444, barrowstreettheatre.com. (Isherwood)
'Full House! The Musical!' This frenetic, ribald and exhausting satire from the creators of "Bayside! The Musical!" barely stops for breath. Only Marshall Louise freezes time with her delicious sendup of the grown Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen. Her impersonation might be one-note, but it is something to treasure (2:00). Theater 80, 80 St. Marks Place, East Village, 212-388-0388, fullhousethemusical.com. (Andy Webster)
* 'Henry IV' This rousing all-female production from the Donmar Warehouse in London makes us listen with newborn ears to the masculine manhood-measuring bravado and insecurity in Shakespeare's portraits of those who would be king. As directed by Phyllida Lloyd, and set as a play-within-a-play in a women's prison, the show becomes a celebration of the metaphoric wonder of live stage acting (2:15). St. Ann's Warehouse, 45 Water Street, Dumbo, Brooklyn, 866-811-4111, stannswarehouse.org. (Brantley)
* 'Hir' A sensational -- in all senses of the word -- dark comedy by the immensely gifted Taylor Mac, about an American family in crisis. What's new? Well, you have to see it to believe it. Kristine Nielsen gives a bravura comic performance as the materfamilias of a clan in the throes of transition, and the rest of the cast is equally fine. Wild, woolly and unforgettable (1:50). Playwrights Horizons, 416 West 42nd Street, 212-279-4200, playwrightshorizons.org. (Isherwood)
* 'The Humans' A middle-class family seems to be spiraling toward a state of perilous entropy in this blisteringly funny, bruisingly sad and altogether wonderful play by Stephen Karam. A flawless cast and equally fine direction by Joe Mantello enhance the power of Mr. Karam's subtle, affecting examination of the way we live now -- which is to say, painfully close to the edge (1:30). Harold and Miriam Steinberg Center for Theater, 111 West 46th Street, 212-719-1300, roundabouttheatre.org. (Isherwood)
'Incident at Vichy' A respectable if sometimes stolid revival of Arthur Miller's 1964 drama about Jews being rounded up for transport to concentration camps. Richard Thomas leads a mostly fine cast in Miller's powerful but speech-laden moral inquiry (1:30). Pershing Square Signature Center, 480 West 42nd Street, 212-244-7529, signaturetheatre.org. (Isherwood)
'Lost Girls' John Pollono ("Small Engine Repair") is a deft practitioner of the sort of twist-in-the-tale narratives associated with short-story writers like W. Somerset Maugham, and this blue-collar family portrait uses a perspective-changing plot turn to warming emotional effect. Jo Bonney directs a very good cast that includes Piper Perabo as an embittered single mother with a runaway daughter (1:30). Lucille Lortel Theater, 121 Christopher Street, West Village, 866-811-4111, mcctheater.org. (Brantley)
'Mad Libs Live!' Despite a likable cast, there is less fun and spontaneity here than the brand name might suggest. Based on the fill-in-the-blank word game, this musical for children 7 and up asks audience members to suggest nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to complete its anodyne songs. The sheer belly laughter of the game comes from surprising juxtapositions. The show plays it too safe, relying on the crowd to supply the silliness (1:00). New World Stages, 340 West 50th Street, 212-239-6200, madlibslive.com. (Laura Collins-Hughes)
'Men Are From Mars, Women Are From Venus, Live!' This show says it's based on John Gray's pop-psychology best seller from the 1990s, and he even appears in two video segments, but it's mostly a monologue by Peter Story. He uses anecdotes from his marriage to convey some of the book's advice and insights about relationships, and he's a great storyteller, but the source material seems even more facile and simplistic now than it did two decades ago (2:00). New World Stages, 340 West 50th Street, 212-239-6200, marsvenuslive.com/newyork. (Neil Genzlinger)
'My Son the Waiter: A Jewish Tragedy' Brad Zimmerman's solo show about almost three decades of working in restaurants while not becoming a famous actor, and his Jewish mother's shame, is low-key, half-familiar, half-mordant and pretty delicious (1:30). Stage 72, 158 West 72nd Street, 212-868-4444, mysonthewaiter.com. (Anita Gates)
* 'Pike St.' In Nilaja Sun's new solo show she portrays three generations of a Puerto Rican family facing an oncoming storm and other pressing problems. Despite the dark subject, Ms. Sun's radiant performance and buoyantly funny writing keep the mood light (1:20). Abrons Arts Center, 466 Grand Street, at Pitt Street, Lower East Side, 866-811-4111, abronsartscenter.org. (Isherwood)
'Ripcord' This expertly engineered situation comedy from David Lindsay-Abaire ("Rabbit Hole," "Good People"), set in an assisted living facility, churns up shadows only to dispel them with punch lines. Directed by David Hyde Pierce, this tale of two adversarial elderly roommates (a perfectly cast Marylouise Burke and Holland Taylor) turns what we're afraid of into gently tickling diversions (2:00). City Center Stage I, 131 West 55th Street, 212-581-1212, ripcordplay.com. (Brantley)
'Ruthless!' This spiked Shirley Temple of a show, which first opened in 1992 and now returns with less fizz, follows the adventures of Tina Denmark (Tori Murray), a stage-struck prepubescent with occasional homicidal tendencies. But what must have once seemed wildly satirical could practically pass for documentary (1:35). St. Luke's Theater, 308 West 46th Street, Clinton, 212-239-6200, ruthlessthemusical.com. (Soloski)
'Shear Madness' This astonishingly durable interactive murder mystery has been running in Boston since Jimmy Carter was in the White House, and some of the jokes in the late-arriving New York incarnation definitely show their years. But the tone of this improv-heavy play depends hugely on the performers. They lend an inclusive spirit of fun to the proceedings, set in a Manhattan hair salon where the audience determines which character is the most likely suspect (2:00). New World Stages, 340 West 50th Street, 212-239-6200, shearmadness.com. (Collins-Hughes)
* 'Songbird' Chicken-fried Chekhov? Sounds dubious, but this musical version of "The Seagull," set in the Nashville music world, makes for one of the more enjoyable updates of the Russian master's great plays. The smart book is by Michael Kimmel, the terrific country score by Lauren Pritchard, and the first-rate cast is led by a radiant Kate Baldwin as the Arkadina figure, here a country music star (2:00). 59E59 Theaters, 59 East 59th Street, 212-279-4200, 59e59.org. (Isherwood)
* '39 Steps' This century's most tireless and high-profile example of the little show that could, adapted from Alfred Hitchcock's 1935 movie and seen on Broadway in 2008, returns for yet another antic struggle with bad guys with bad accents. Directed by Maria Aitken, with a cast of four playing too many roles to count, this paradigm of bare-bones theater remains indomitably funny (1:40). Union Square Theater, 100 East 17th Street, Gramercy, 877-250-2929, 39stepsny.com. (Brantley)
'Trip of Love' This bloated concoction takes a seemingly random collection of Top 40 hits from the 1960s and turns them into dance numbers, with a sparse, cliched girl-meets-boy story woven in. Ridiculous stuff, but the big-budget sets are sometimes fun (1:50). Stage 42, 422 West 42nd Street,212-239-6200, tripoflove.com. (Genzlinger)
* 'Ugly Lies the Bone' Lindsey Ferrentino's bracing, powerful drama about a severely wounded war veteran, played with grit and dry humor by a superb Mamie Gummer, confronts an achingly topical issue with hardheaded honesty and admirable compassion (1:15). Harold and Miriam Steinberg Center for Theater, Black Box Theater, 111 West 46th Street, 212-719-1300, roundabouttheatre.org. (Isherwood)
Extravaganzas
* 'Big Apple Circus: The Grand Tour' Friendly, beguiling and emphatically humane, this popular circus takes travel as its theme this year, with its performers cavorting as Jazz Age sailors, aviators and sightseers. The aerial act is terrific, the horses elegant, the Wheel of Wonder sufficiently terrifying. Many of the acts are graceful rather than thrilling, but this is nevertheless a first-class jaunt. And it only takes a MetroCard to get there (2:00). Damrosch Park, Lincoln Center; 800-922-3772, bigapplecircus.org. (Soloski)
Long-Running Shows
'Aladdin' The Disney movie refashioned for the stage, with shtick, sparkles and silliness cutting the syrup (2:20). New Amsterdam Theater, 214 West 42nd Street, 866-870-2717, aladdinthemusical.com.
'Avenue Q' R-rated puppets give lively life lessons (2:15). New World Stages, 340 West 50th Street, Clinton, 212-239-6200, telecharge.com.

Last Chance
'The Astronaut Love Show' (closes on Sunday) This musical satire by Fred Sauter and Paul Leschen ("Bedbugs!!!") commandeers three real-life stories of obsession: the German cannibal who ate his willing victim, the astronaut who challenged her romantic rival in an airport parking lot and the besotted blonde who may or may not have kidnapped a Mormon missionary. John de los Santos's manic production lunges for laughs, but the parts of the show that work best go deeper than mere ridicule (1:40). Kraine Theater, 85 East Fourth Street, East Village, astronautloveshow.com. (Collins-Hughes)
'Captive Audience: An Evening of Short Plays by David Ives' (closes on Sunday) This program of seven short plays by David Ives is presented by New York Deaf Theater and employs both spoken English and American Sign Language to tell its comedic tales (2:00). June Havoc Theater, Abingdon Theater Arts Complex, 312 West 36th Street, 212-868-4444, newyorkdeaftheatre.org. (Ken Jaworowski)
'Chang(e)' (closes on Sunday) This docudrama by Soomi Kim and Suzi Takahashi is about an activist artist named Kathy Change, who died by self-immolation in 1996 at the University of Pennsylvania. Oh, is she a tough subject -- not just because research and interviews have resulted in only a fragmentary portrait but also because she was something of a campus crank. Onstage, she's as easy for us to tune out as she is for the students around her (1:30). Here, 145 Avenue of the Americas, at Dominick Street, South Village, 212-352-3101, here.org. (Collins-Hughes)
* 'Charles Francis Chan Jr.'s Exotic Oriental Murder Mystery' (closes on Saturday) Lloyd Suh's very messy, very funny, very angry play inspects and implodes how Asian-Americans have been characterized and caricatured. The material could stand tightening and tautening, but the cast is excellent, the themes trenchant and the style antic and impassioned (2:30). Walkerspace, 46 Walker Street, TriBeCa, 866-811-4111, naatco.org. (Soloski)
* 'The Comedy of Errors' (closes on Sunday) This touring production from the Public Theater's Mobile Shakespeare Unit offers an incredibly mobile take on Shakespeare's early, antic comedy of identical twins on the loose. Seven performers, in an assortment of headgear, play a multitude of roles in an exhilaratingly footloose production, directed by Kwame Kwei-Armah, that never, ever stands still (1:30). Public Theater, 425 Lafayette Street, at Astor Place, East Village, 212-967-7555, publictheater.org. (Brantley)
'Dead and Breathing' (closes on Monday) Chisa Hutchinson's two-hander about an ailing woman and the Christian nurse she tries to bribe into killing her is a series of ethical skirmishes, but not only about the right to die. Privilege faces off against want, Christianity against nonbelief, and it all feels very engineered. By the time Ms. Hutchinson introduces a debate over transgender issues, she has long since passed the point of piling on (1:20). National Black Theater, 2031 Fifth Avenue, near 126th Street, East Harlem, 866-811-4111, nationalblacktheatre.org. (Collins-Hughes)
'Death of a Salesman' (closes on Sunday) Thanks to the fineness of the performances and the clarity of the English supertitles, language is no barrier for a non-Yiddish speaker in this New Yiddish Rep production of Arthur Miller's play, adapted by Joseph Buloff. These are Lomans fit to bruise some hearts. So it's frustrating that design, of all things, is what fatally hobbles this staging (2:30). Castillo Theater, 543 West 42nd Street, Clinton, 866-811-4111, castillo.org. (Collins-Hughes)
* 'First Daughter Suite' (closes on Sunday) Michael John LaChiusa's four-part oratorio about the female residents of the White House might be described as compassionate political satire, an unlikely hybrid of tabloid triviality and emotional depth. Directed by Kirsten Sanderson, the witty, vocally agile cast includes Mary Testa, Carly Tamer and a priceless Alison Fraser (as both Betty Ford and Nancy Reagan) (2:30). Public Theater, 425 Lafayette Street at Astor Place, East Village, 212-967-7555, publictheater.org. (Brantley)
* 'Futurity' (closes on Sunday) An ambitious, frisky furrowed brow of a musical with book, brainy lyrics and music by César Alvarez (with his band the Lisps), who also stars as a Civil War soldier who dreams up a peacemaking machine with the help of the pioneering British mathematician Ada Lovelace (Sammy Tunis) (1:50). Connelly Theater, 220 East Fourth Street, East Village, 866-811-4111, sohorep.org, arsnovanyc.com. (Isherwood)
'Houseworld' (closes on Saturday) This latest example of environmental theater transforms a Brooklyn mission house into a D.I.Y. dreamscape for role-playing and soul-baring. It is described by its creators as "a safe space for the audience to express sides of themselves they usually repress." Mostly, that mandate is translated into something like a frat house party with a Zen side (2:30). San Damiano Mission, 85 North 15th Street, Greenpoint, Brooklyn, houseworld.nyc. (Brantley)
'Professor Brenner' (closes on Sunday) There are a lot of contemporary themes in this 1911 play by David Pinski about a 61-year-old man who frets about his romance with a 20-year-old woman. The production, though, is marred by a whiny rendition of the title character; no woman of any age could love such an insecure wimp (1:10). Here, 145 Avenue of the Americas, at Dominick Street, South Village, 212-352-3101, here.org. (Genzlinger)
'tiger tiger (on the nature of violence)' (closes on Saturday) Jessica Almasy's play has lots of roar and little bite. Ostensibly based on a real-life zoo mauling on Christmas Day in 2007, it begins in a reasonably coherent and engaging fashion. Then Girl Scouts, group therapy and a singing molar get involved (1:25). Dixon Place, 161A Chrystie Street at Rivington, 866-811-4111, dixonplace.org. (Soloski)
* 'Who's Your Baghdaddy? Or How I Started the Iraq War' (closes on Sunday) A cunning, rock-solid musical comedy (with the just the right amount of absurdism) that explains the war better than anything else has so far (2:00). Actors Temple Theater, 339 West 47th Street, 212-239-6200, whoisyourbaghdaddy.com. (Gates)

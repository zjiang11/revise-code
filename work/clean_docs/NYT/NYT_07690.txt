Ratings and running times are in parentheses; foreign films have English subtitles. Full reviews of all current releases: nytimes.com/movies.
'Bears' (G, 1:18) Disney's latest animal documentary looks at one hungry year in the life of an Alaska bear and her two cubs. It's not the most dazzling work of nature photography, but the furry stars hold a visual fascination throughout. (Nicolas Rapold)
* 'Bicycling With Moliere' (No rating, 1:44, in French) In Philippe Le Guay's witty contemporary deconstruction of Molière's comedy "The Misanthrope," two actors of opposite temperament discuss a possible collaboration on a new production of the play. Their conversations are part of a sneaky power game in which each tries to get the best of the other. (Stephen Holden)
'Blue Ruin' (R, 1:30) Selling its empty calories with great skill, Jeremy Saulnier's seductively photographed revenge thriller follows a murderous homeless man (Macon Blair) and his hillbilly marks through a screenplay that mocks vigilante violence while slapping it on in gorgeous splatters. (Jeannette Catsoulis)
'Brick Mansions' (PG-13, 1:30) This Detroit-set remake of the French action movie "District B13" is fast and loud, as it should be -- with plenty of car chases and parkour -- but it also has a tantalizing hint of class consciousness and a dollop of streetwise wit, mostly thanks to RZA of the Wu-Tang Clan, who plays a drug kingpin with unusual priorities. (A.O. Scott)
'Captain America: The Winter Soldier' (PG-13, 2:16) Given how little creative wiggle room there is in properties like "The Winter Soldier," a sequel to "Captain America: The First Avenger" (2011), it counts as a minor triumph that the directors, the brothers Anthony and Joe Russo, imprint any personality on this new movie, which is less a stand-alone work than a part of an ever-expanding multimedia enterprise. As the World War II veteran turned contemporary superhero, Chris Evans remains a recessive if appealing screen presence. (Manohla Dargis)
'Dancing in Jaffa' (No rating, 1:30, in English, Arabic and Hebrew) In this documentary, Pierre Dulaine, a former champion in ballroom dancing and the founder of Dancing Classrooms, heads to Israel to try to instill respect among 10- and 11-year-old Jewish and Palestinian Israelis through a course in the movements (and courtesies) of Latin dance. A climactic competition is irresistible, while a closing coda is almost too pat. But Mr. Dulaine is a warm presence, and the kids are delightful. (Andy Webster)
'Divergent' (PG-13, 2:23) Shailene Woodley stars as Tris Prior in this adaptation of the first book in Veronica Roth's young adult series. It's another dystopian tale that pivots on a gutsy teenage heroine, one living in a postwar Chicago. For all its similarities to "The Hunger Games," "Divergent" isn't as interesting partly because Tris spends a lot of time wondering why her cute male instructor pays attention to her: The story celebrates individuality and breaking out of the little boxes that its authoritarian leaders (i.e., adults) put teenagers in, even as it sticks to the usual gender template. The director, Neal Burger, gives you little to hang on to -- beauty, thrills, a visual style. (Dargis)
'Draft Day' (PG-13, 1:49) This promotional film for the N.F.L. is nothing special, but it is redeemed somewhat by Ivan Reitman's smooth direction and the leathery professionalism of Kevin Costner as a Cleveland Brown's general manager facing many tough decisions. (Scott)
* 'Ernest & Celestine' (PG, 1:20, in either French or English) A tale of mice and bears, derring-do and dentistry, this lovely animated movie about would-be foes who become friends originated in a cycle of children's books by the Belgian writer and artist Gabrielle Vincent. The books have simple stories and enchanting illustrations by Vincent that are characterized by graceful lines, muted colors and blurred edges. The screen character designs are broader and more overtly comic, but the three directors -- Benjamin Renner, Vincent Patar and Stéphane Aubier -- have retained enough of Vincent's charming vision that the movie feels intimate and personal. (Dargis)
'Fading Gigolo' (R, 1:30) John Turturro directed, and stars in, this low-key, low-energy comedy as a former bookstore employee turned prostitute whose pimp is played by none other than a deftly funny Woody Allen. It's a story of New York haves and have-nots that never manages to persuade, despite the engaging supporting likes of Sharon Stone, Sofía Vergara, Vanessa Paradis and especially Liev Schreiber as a hunky Hasidic neighborhood cop. (Dargis)
'Finding Vivian Maier' (No rating, 1:23) An exciting electric current of discovery runs through John Maloof and Charlie Siskel's documentary about the street photographer who worked as a care provider and never exhibited her work. Maier is a terrific story -- part Mary Poppins, part Weegee -- and the movie is a solid if finally thin introduction to her. It's also, to state the obvious, a feature-length advertisement for Mr. Maloof's commercial venture as the principal owner of her work and would have been stronger if it had dug into the complexities of what it means when one person assumes ownership of another's art. (Dargis)
'For No Good Reason' (No rating, 1:29) Charlie Paul's admiring documentary about Ralph Steadman, the British artist and illustrator, reflects on his work and his long association with Hunter S. Thompson. Mr. Paul clearly admires his subject, but his reliance on celebrity testimony (mostly from Johnny Depp) and irrelevant pop songs suggests that he doesn't trust that the audience will share his interest. (Scott)
'The French Minister (Quai D'Orsay)' (No rating, 1:53, in French) Fires burn, simmer and die, only to rekindle, in Bertrand Tavernier's sharp, sly comedy of manners and errors. The movie is based on "Quai d'Orsay," a graphic novel illustrated by Christophe Blain, whose sharp caricatures and comic-book flourishes Mr. Tavernier has nicely translated to the screen. The book's writer, Abel Lanzac, is a pseudonym for Antonin Baudry, a diplomat who based it on his experiences working for Dominique de Villepin (a terrific Thierry Lhermitte plays the fictionalized version), the foreign minister during the lead-up to the war in Iraq. (Dargis)
'The German Doctor' (PG-13, 1:33, in Spanish and German) Set in Patagonia in the 1960s, this quietly jittery art house thriller hinges on a relationship that a young girl, Lilith (Florencia Bado), forms with a stranger (Alex Brendemühl, suave and icy) who turns out to be Josef Mengele. The Argentine writer and director Lucía Puenzo, shooting in wide screen, takes an effective, largely cool approach to her fictionalization of Mengele's time in South America. She overworks some of her more blatant visual motifs, but the blond children at a local German school unsettle the mood nicely. (Dargis)
* 'The Grand Budapest Hotel' (R, 1:39) Wes Anderson's latest film is whimsical, fussy and ingenious -- a Wes Anderson film, in other words -- but it is also a sly and touching engagement with some of the ugliest parts of 20th-century European history. Ralph Fiennes plays a hotel concierge in the fictional Central-European republic of Zubrowka in the fateful year of 1932. The frames are beautifully composed, the production design exquisite and a large cast is dispatched on a complex caper. But all the fun is shadowed by -- and is a brilliant protest against -- some very cruel realities. (Scott)
'Hateship Loveship' (R, 1:44) Adapted from a story by Alice Munro, Liza Johnson's film is a well-observed, finely acted tale of secrets and surprises, with Kristen Wiig as a quiet housekeeper whose buried passions collide with the dysfunctional lives of her employers. The movie slackens and softens as it goes along, but Ms. Wiig and the rest of the cast (including Nick Nolte, Hailee Steinfeld, Guy Pearce and Christine Lahti) are always interesting to watch. (Scott)
'A Haunted House 2' (R, 1:26) This parody franchise from Marlon Wayans, after only two entries, already displays a sputtering exhaustion. Though he enlists support from the likes of Jaime Pressly, Gabriel Iglesias, the talented Missi Pyle and Cedric the Entertainer (back from the first "Haunted House"), this is Mr. Wayans's show, and his perspiring physicality, wide-eyed mugging and nonstop pop culture references grow exasperating. (Webster)
'Heaven Is for Real' (PG, 1:40) Working with hushed choirs, tasteful beams of light and an ethereal aesthetic, Randall Wallace directs this verdant prairie poem (based on a 2010 book) about a hard-up Nebraska pastor (Greg Kinnear) whose small son surfaces from a ruptured appendix insisting that he saw seraphim and a horse-riding Jesus. (Catsoulis)
* 'Jodorowsky's Dune' (No rating, 1:30, in English, French, German and Spanish) Cheerfully partial and unapologetically deferential, Frank Pavich's account of the Chilean director Alejandro Jodorowsky's failed efforts to film Frank Herbert's science fiction novel "Dune" bristles with offbeat talent, revelatory art work and operatic self-promotion. (Catsoulis)
'Last Passenger' (R, 1:37) Minimally plotted and modestly executed, Omid Nooshin's quintessentially English action movie -- no sex, lots of talking and not a six-pack in sight -- places a disparate group of people on a runaway commuter train with limited access to special effects. (Catsoulis)
'Locke' (R, 1:25) Steven Knight's movie about a man in lockdown -- emotionally, psychologically, existentially -- asks you to spend 85 minutes in intimate quarters with Tom Hardy, who's mostly seen in medium close-up behind a steering wheel. It's a story about a man on the verge of making a life-changing decision and, like a lot of road movies, the journey is the destination. Moment by moment, with a twitch, a shudder, a look, Mr. Hardy movingly draws you in, turning a stranger's face into a life. (Dargis)
* 'The Lunchbox' (PG, 1:44, in Hindi) Ritesh Batra's charming debut, set in Mumbai, refreshes an old and sturdy conceit (perhaps most familiar from "You've Got Mail"). Two lonely souls -- a neglected young housewife (Nimrat Kaur) and a middle-aged, widowed office worker (Irrfan Khan) -- pursue an anonymous correspondence that begins when a home-cooked lunch is delivered to the wrong place. (Scott)
* 'Manakamana' (No rating, 1:58, in Nepali and English) Stephanie Spray and Pacho Velez's transporting ethnographic film, set in a green sliver of Nepal, consists of 11 uncut shots, most taken in a closed cable car. It's a fascinating trip filled with men, women, children and the occasional animal who make for as gripping viewing as the landscape unfolding behind them. For 10 or so minutes at a time, each floats hundreds of feet above a lush forest, traveling to and from the Hindu temple that gives this film its rhythmic title. By focusing on such a narrow slice of life, Ms. Spray and Mr. Velez have ceded any totalizing claim on the truth and instead settled for a perfect incompleteness. Essential viewing. (Dargis)
'Mr. Peabody & Sherman' (PG, 1:32) A dog and his boy, traveling through time. This latest bit of big-studio baby boomer pop-culture recycling is genial and witty, though not quite as aggressively brainy as the original Jay Ward animation. The goofy high spirits are marred by the kind of unthinking sexism that, even in the wake of "Brave" and "Frozen," remains the default setting for feature-length animation. (Scott)
'Muppets Most Wanted' (PG, 1:47) In this follow-up to the 2011 Muppet movie, Kermit lands in a gulag while an evil frog who bears a striking resemblance to him takes his place on an international Muppet tour. The charm of the earlier film is replaced with plotting complexities, and the movie has more grown-up jokes than its predecessor. It's sometimes funny but rarely lovable. (Neil Genzlinger)
'Noah' (PG-13, 2:18) Darren Aronofsky's version of the familiar Sunday school story is a scary, brooding environmental allegory, in which Noah (Russell Crowe) takes his divine instructions as a warrant for fanatical, almost genocidal behavior. Noah, assisted by stone giants, is pitted against Tubal-Cain (Ray Winstone), who wants to save corrupt humanity from its creator's wrath. The movie is clumsy and not always coherent, but at its mad best it is a powerful, sincere attempt to find modern meanings in the Old Testament. (Scott)
'Non-Stop' (PG-13, 1:46) A satisfying, primitive bluntness distinguishes "Non-Stop," which turns on an air marshal (Liam Neeson) who, during a super-bad flight, tries to stop an anonymous extortionist from killing a passenger every 20 minutes. The story is as nonsensical as it sounds, but the film's director, Jaume Collet-Serra, has a sure genre hand and real feeling for what Mr. Neeson brings to the screen at this stage of his career. (Dargis)
'Nymphomaniac: Volume I' (No rating, 1:58) The title of Lars von Trier's latest would-be provocation is preposterous, a huckster gimmick; it may also be a dig at those who, I think wrongly, label him a misogynist because of the abuse he rains down on his female characters. Women suffer in his work, yet they also dominate, shape and haunt it. That's true of Joe (Charlotte Gainsbourg) who, over the course of this fitfully amusing sometimes explicit demi-movie, relates her sex life to a stranger (Stellan Skarsgard). And then the movie bluntly ends and the credits roll next to some teasing clips from "Volume II" that imply Joe's sexcapades are about to turn very ugly or deeply silly, both being possible with Mr. von Trier. (Dargis)
'Nymphomaniac: Volume II' (No rating, 2:03) In the second half of Lars von Trier's four-hour sex movie, Joe (Charlotte Gainsbourg) recounts a set of erotic adventures that are not as much fun -- for her or the audience -- as the ones in "Volume I." Less scandalous than dull and pompous -- in effect a long mansplaining of female sexuality -- this installment nonetheless delivers a nasty and eminently von Trierian punch line at the very end. (Scott)
'Oculus' (R, 1:44) This derivative but efficient chiller that cribs from "Solaris," "The Shining" and "The Amityville Horror" also shows glimmers of imagination. The elegant wide-screen, however, can't totally obscure stiff acting. (Ben Kenigsberg)
'Only Lovers Left Alive' (R, 2:03) Tilda Swinton and Tom Hiddleston are the coolest vampires ever in Jim Jarmusch's latest movie, a seductive tribute to its own aesthetic preoccupations. (Scott)
'The Other Woman' (PG-13, 1:49) Cameron Diaz has her hands full with this female revenge comedy, a shrill, lazy, clumsily assembled rip-off of "The First Wives Club" that could crush any actor forced to execute its leaden slapstick gags and mouth its crude humorless dialogue. (Holden)
* 'Particle Fever' (No rating, 1:39) This documentary, directed by Mark Levinson, chronicles the work of theoretical and experimental physicists involved with the Large Hadron Collider, an enormous gizmo near Geneva that in 2012 isolated the elusive Higgs boson (a subatomic morsel believed to hold the key to understanding the universe). If that sentence is clear to you, you should buy a ticket now, but even if the intricacies of physics lie beyond your grasp, the grandeur of the enterprise and the humanity of the scientists themselves make for mind-blowing viewing. (Scott)
'The Quiet Ones' (PG-13, 1:38) In this latest horror movie about weird doings in the 1970s, Jared Harris stars as an Oxford University professor who believes the supernatural occurrences in a patient's bleak, lonely life have been created by her mind and is using every bad idea he has -- including curing her through sleep deprivation -- to prove it. As in other horror tales with doctors and scientists, the darkness emerges during the struggle between an ostensibly rational mind and the creatures that are manifestations of irrationality and, by their very existence, emblems of religious faith. Too bad it isn't scary. (Dargis)
'The Raid 2' (R, 2:28, in Indonesian) The pleasant surprise of Gareth Evans's sturdy sequel to "The Raid: Redemption" is that neither its undercover drama nor its long running time bog down its bracing, expertly staged fight fests. Iko Uwais returns as Rama, a principled Jakarta cop who infiltrates a crime syndicate by aiding the boss's son. (Rapold)
* 'The Railway Man' (R, 1:56) Is there a place today for a solid, old-fashioned movie about war, remembrance and reconciliation? This unabashedly stodgy, high-minded film in the David Lean tradition (but minus the grandeur), stars Colin Firth, giving a devastating understated portrayal of Eric Lomax, a British Army officer who was captured and tortured by the Japanese during World War II. (Holden)
'Rio 2' (G, 1:41) Rejoining the pair of blue macaws (still voiced by Jesse Eisenberg and Anne Hathaway) from his previous feature, Carlos Saldanha's frantic musical smothers its pops of avant-garde flavor in the retro rhythms of comic formula and the relentless beats of choreographed stimulation. (Catsoulis)
'Rob the Mob' (R, 1:44) Bright and fleet, Raymond De Felitta's breezy retelling of one of New York City's more unusual crime sprees follows a pair of crazy-in-love and comically dumb crooks (Michael Pitt and Nina Arianda) as they rob a series of Mafia social clubs. (Catsoulis)
'Transcendence' (PG-13, 1:59) Johnny Depp is weirdly perfect as an artificial-intelligence genius who becomes the monster of his own creation or so it seems after his consciousness is uploaded onto the Internet. Directed by Wally Pfister, a cinematographer making his feature directing debut, this inelegant, no doubt implausible (maybe not) science-fiction film is a dark, lurchingly entertaining pastiche of age-old worries, future-shock jolts, hot-button topics and old-fashioned genre thrills. (Dargis)
'2 States' (No rating, 2:15, in Hindi) Can a Punjabi boy and a Tamilian girl find happiness together? Not if they can't make peace between their families. Abhishek Varman's Bollywood comedy has some novelistic texture -- it was adapted from a book by Chetan Bhagat, but it doesn't have much dramatic punch. It sags, and the characters don't have enough interesting edges to prop it up. (Rachel Saltz)
* 'Under the Skin' (R, 1:48) In this somber Kubrickian sci-fi horror movie, Scarlett Johansson plays a predatory alien who assumes a human identity and lures men to their deaths. Jonathan Glazer's absorbing movie leaves you reflecting on the notion that every being in the universe is an alien in disguise. (Holden)
'Walking With the Enemy' (No rating, 2:04) This fictionalized account is based on the real-life deeds of Pinchas Rosenbaum, a Hungarian rabbi's son who was able to save Jews from the Nazis and their collaborators by disguising himself in enemy garb. The movie shows once again that the imperatives of melodrama and the indifferent slaughter of the Holocaust make for an uneasy mix. (Kenigsberg)
* 'Watermark' (PG, 1:32) Primal, striking and often frustratingly oblique, Jennifer Baichwal and Edward Burtynsky's second investigation into the impact of human beings on the natural world travels across 10 countries and countless human activities to observe a species in thrall to H2O and largely heedless of the consequences of its unnatural manipulation. (Catsoulis)
* 'Le Week-End' (R, 1:33) Nick (Jim Broadbent) and Meg (Lindsay Duncan), a left-leaning, academic British baby boomer couple, arrive in Paris to celebrate their 30th anniversary. But are we witnessing the celebration of a long marriage or its sudden unraveling? The writer Hanif Kureishi and the director Roger Michell (whose previous collaborations include "The Mother" and "The Buddha of Suburbia") confect what is on the surface a bittersweet comedy of love in maturity. But there are strong, angry passions under the surface (particularly as far as Meg is concerned) and an eruption of sharp social satire when Jeff Goldblum shows up playing an old graduate school chum of Nick's who has matured into a happy and prosperous sellout. (Scott)
'Who Is Dayani Cristal?' (No rating, 1:25) A kind of forensic memorial, Marc Silver's documentary pieces together the background of a Central American migrant worker who died trying to cross into the United States. With its multifaceted approach, its earnest compassion and a celebrity presence in the form of the actor Gael García Bernal, the movie could be enlisted in the perennial battle over immigration law, but its execution presents problems not containable by the filmmakers' noble intention. (Rapold)
'The Wind Rises' (No rating, 2:06, in Japanese) Hayao Miyazaki's newest (and reportedly final) film tells the fictionalized story of Jiro Horikoshi, a gifted aeronautic engineer who is historically notable -- or infamous -- for designing deadly war planes used by Japan in World War II. Mr. Miyazaki's elegantly lyrical film chronicles Horikoshi's process of invention and his poignant romance with his tubercular wife, shadowed by dreamlike premonitions and creative visions. (Rapold)
'Young & Beautiful' (No rating, 1:35) François Ozon's coming-of-age story, in which an upper-middle-class French high school student (Marine Vacth) takes up prostitution as an extracurricular activity, is coy and prurient rather than illuminating, and oddly dull even when it tries to be provocative. (Scott)
Film Series
Mizoguchi (Friday through June 8) We have only seven of the 123 plays that Sophocles wrote and 30 of the 85 films that Kenji Mizoguchi directed. Mizoguchi may have the higher percentage of surviving pieces, but he also worked his rigorous, haunting magic about 2,300 years after Sophocles did -- and losing 55 films even half as good as Mizoguchi's "Sansho the Bailiff" and "Ugetsu" is heartbreaking. Museum of the Moving Image's essential retrospective will feature those this weekend, as well as several films that have rarely been seen in the United States, including "Song of Home" (Sunday at 2 p.m.), his earliest existing film, and "Oyuki the Virgin" (Sunday at 3:30 p.m.), based on a Guy de Maupassant story about two "fallen women" (a pet subject for Mizoguchi) who teach the higher classes a few things about morality. 35th Avenue at 37th Street, Astoria, Queens, 718-784-0077, movingimage.us; $12. (Eric Grode)
New York African Film Festival (Wednesday through May 13) The 21st edition of this annual festival, devotes its opening night to "Confusion Na Wa" (Wednesday at 7:30 p.m.), a dark comedy from that country that took top honors at last year's African Movie Academy Awards. Political themes play a big role this year, sometimes overtly (in the Zimbabwean documentary "Mugabe: Villain or Hero?," screening Wednesday at 2 p.m. and May 11 at 6:15 p.m.) and sometimes in more coded ways (the tormented teacher in the grisly South African neo-noir "Of Good Report," screening on May 10 at 6:30 p.m. and May 12 at 9 p.m.). Another highlight is the New York premiere of the film adaptation of Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's novel "Half of a Yellow Sun" (starring Thandie Newton, Anika Noni Rose and Chiwetel Ejiofor) next Friday at 7 and 9:45 p.m., a week before its official opening. Elinor Bunin Munroe Film Center, Lincoln Center, 144 West 65th Street, Manhattan, 212-875-5601, filmlinc.com; $13. (Grode)
'Othello' (through Thursday) How many Desdemonas does it take to make a visually rapturous, Palme d'Or-winning Shakespeare adaptation? Three, if you're Orson Welles. He had to film over three years while he lined up new backers, sometimes even sponsoring stage productions of the work to raise funds. (One such production inspired him to dub the entire performance of Suzanne Cloutier, his third and final Desdemona, with his onstage leading lady.) When the film opened in New York in 1955 -- six years after filming began -- Bosley Crowther dismissed it in The New York Times as a "tumbled, slurred and helter-skelter film" and "just a little Shakespeare and a lot of Welles." For those who take that as an enticement, this new restoration is for you. Film Forum, 209 West Houston Street, west of Avenue of the Americas, South Village, 212-727-8110, filmforum.org; $13. (Grode)
Punk Rock Girls (Wednesday through June 1) BAMcinématek's 12-film survey of women who know how to make some noise kicks off on Wednesday, at 7:30 p.m., with Lukas Moodysson's latest, "We Are the Best!" Mr. Moodysson will also appear at the screening of the film, about three 13-year-old girls who discover punk rock in 1980s Sweden. Many of the directors of the other films in this series surrounded their stars -- including Diane Lane ("Ladies and Gentlemen, the Fabulous Stains," on May 31), Rosario Dawson (the surprisingly subversive "Josie and the Pussycats," on May 29) and Rosanna Arquette ("Desperately Seeking Susan," on June 1) -- with real-life punk rockers like Siouxsie Sioux, Richard Hell and the Slits. Also appearing at the festival will be Slava Tsukerman, who directed the 1982 cult classic "Liquid Sky," which Mr. Tsukerman will introduce on May 30. He also co-wrote its ominous, synth-heavy score, considered by many to be the jumping-off point for electroclash music. BAM Rose Cinemas, 30 Lafayette Avenue, at Ashland Place, Fort Greene, Brooklyn, 718-636-4100, bam.org; $13. (Grode)

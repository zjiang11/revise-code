The scale, reach and fluidity of the Internet are reshaping supplier networks with a level of sophistication not foreseen even by the most ardent pioneers of online business-to-business exchanges.
Back in the deranged days of dot-com mania, a company called Ariba could do no wrong.
Ariba promised to revolutionize the way businesses bought things. It would create a vast network linking buyers and sellers, so companies could purchase toilet paper, office chairs, corrugated boxes and even custodial services with unheard-of efficiency. The Internet had arrived, and purchase orders, faxes and buying consultants stood no chance against a vibrant, digitized marketplace.
For a while, Ariba, based in Sunnyvale, California, was one of Silicon Valley's hottest properties. In March 2000, its share price soared past $1,000, adjusted for two rapid-fire share splits.
Unfortunately, there was a problem with Ariba's business model. The company was a thing of beauty in theory, but few in its target audience -- the world's businesses -- bothered to use it.
"They built the network, but nobody came," recalled Dana Gardner, the principal analyst at Interarbor Solutions, a technology research company.
By September 2001, Ariba's share price had collapsed to $2.20. It and about a dozen other companies that had vowed to build wondrous trading networks were cast aside and left for dead.
But unlike most of its peers, Ariba did something remarkable: It survived. So, too, did the concept of business-to-business exchanges.
And today, the scale, reach and fluidity of the Internet have started to reshape supplier networks with a level of sophistication not foreseen even by the zealous pundits of 10 years ago. On Ariba's exchange, companies deal in items as varied as steel, fats, name badges, pickles, solvents, taco seasoning and cardboard. Increasingly, companies trade in services, too, placing bids for legal and janitorial work, for example.
Ariba's network links about 1,000 buyers and 300,000 sellers. In the past 12 months, about $135 billion worth of goods and services have been sold on the system, up from about $115 billion in the previous 12 months. Earlier this month, Ariba paid $150 million to acquire Quadrem and its network of 70,000 suppliers, which sold $30 billion worth of goods last year to customers like Alcoa, Rio Tinto and Nestle.
Large companies like Ford Motor and Procter & Gamble have long used homemade and purchased technology to refine their supply chains and to narrow their supplier lists. It is all part of improving business basics, like understanding what you buy and how you buy it.
The networks open more options for these giants, and the pitch from companies like Ariba and CombineNet, a specialist in complex transactions, is that littler guys can get in on this goods-and-services dealing and save money.
"If you can't grow the top line, you can save money through improving bottom-line business efficiency issues like managing cash flow," Mr. Gardner said. "That has been a big incentive to look at this type of electronic commerce."
Why are companies now flocking to technologies they disregarded a decade ago? Although trading exchanges sounded great back then, most companies were not prepared to engage in furious trading for their crucial supplies.
"In 1999 and 2000, it was nothing more than an idea," said Robert M. Calderoni, Ariba's chief executive. "There was no substance behind the idea."
Businesses had yet to refine the internal technology systems needed to get their own houses in order. So the idea of performing complex reverse auctions with other companies fell somewhere between daunting and horrifying.
Given these constraints, Ariba reinvented itself as a seller of software to help companies analyze buying decisions. Jonathan Kirby, chief procurement officer at AstraZeneca, the pharmaceutical giant, said these techniques were a must today.
"You want to know what you spend with whom and what you pay for it," Mr. Kirby said. "Without that information, we couldn't exert our influence and bring cost reductions."
As companies bought into this idea, Ariba slowly coaxed them onto its network and eventually reached a critical mass, said Robert M. Calderoni, Ariba's chief executive, said.
Procter & Gamble exemplifies how companies can deploy buying technology in all kinds of artful ways. It uses what amounts to an artificial intelligence system, developed by CombineNet, to manage the $1 billion it spends every year with trucking companies.
CombineNet's software analyzes truck routes, companies' prices and on-time arrival rates. In essence, it can weigh cost alongside customer service, providing a level of refinement not seen before.
Rick Hughes, chief purchasing officer at Procter & Gamble, says suppliers now compete in all kinds of new ways because of the exchanges' sophistication. Consider the design of the company's bottles.
"In the past, we would design a bottle, take it out to the suppliers, find out what it will cost and try to negotiate that down," he said. "Now, we have access to much broader scale and can ask suppliers what kinds of innovations they can bring to, say, optimize a bottle of Tide."
As Ariba and CombineNet, based in Pittsburgh, have moved to lower-cost models and improved technology, they have fostered more varied transactions.
Three years ago, CombineNet's average deal was $70 million, said Rich Wilson, its president. "That is coming down dramatically," he said, with some deals now under $1 million.
With base networks now in place, analysts like Mr. Gardner expect trades to become, well, weird. Along with trading, say, toilet paper, they are beginning to trade financial contracts with third parties based on expected sales of toilet paper and the creditworthiness of the customer buying the rolls.
"I hate to say it, but it becomes like a derivatives market," Mr. Gardner said. "People are starting to trade accounts receivable as if they were a financial instrument."

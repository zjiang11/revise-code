CORRECTION APPENDED'TUBBY THE TUBA'
He's big, awkward and ungainly. He's mocked by his peers. He worries that he sounds funny.
He could be a bullied child, but he's actually a musical instrument: Tubby the Tuba, misunderstood hero of the piece that bears his name. But while Tubby is not, orchestrally speaking, a rock star, on Saturday his story will be told by someone who is: Patti Smith, below, the singer and poet.
"When I was a child in the '50s, there was actually a cartoon version we used to get to see," Ms. Smith, 66, said in a phone interview. "I just loved it, and it was probably my first introduction to a more classical kind of music."
Ms. Smith will help today's children get that introduction in "Tubby the Tuba," part of the Little Orchestra Society's series Happy Concerts for Young People, for ages 6 through 12. She will narrate this 1940s work, whose text, by Paul Tripp, chronicles Tubby's embarrassment. But then a bullfrog shows him that deep voices can make lovely melodies too. George Kleinsinger's score captures both Tubby's humiliation, in the skittering laughs of the violins and derisive hooting of the trumpets, and his triumph when he sings a stirring, resonant theme all his own.
In her 2010 memoir, "Just Kids," Ms. Smith recounts her joyful rediscovery of the sheet music for "Tubby" in the home of Kleinsinger, her neighbor in the Chelsea Hotel. When the Little Orchestra Society called on her, she said she was excited to give life to the character, "a wallflower kind of kid" like her.
"Tubby in the end is an artist," Ms. Smith said. "We're drawn to Tubby because he's an outsider, and he doesn't seem to have a place, but he finds a way to create something that inspires everyone."
The concert, led by the guest conductor David Alan Miller, will also include excerpts from pieces by Glinka, Haydn, Mozart, Tan Dun and Morton Gould. Soyoung Park will sing the Queen of the Night aria from "The Magic Flute," and the dancer Lane Alexander will solo in Gould's "Tap Dance Concerto." "Tubby " will be the finale, and while Ms. Smith doesn't perform professionally for children, she expects to connect with her audience.
"I'm the quintessential 11-year-old, really," she said. "I'm still the girl who used to go out in the forest with her dog. No matter where I am in my life, I know where she is."
(Saturday, 11 a.m. and 1 p.m., Avery Fisher Hall, Lincoln Center, 212-971-9500, littleorchestra.org; $15 to $55.) LAUREL GRAEBER

'The Adventures of Maya the Bee' (Saturday) This feisty puppet heroine has buzzed back to the Culture Project, where her journey began a dozen years ago. Based on a children's book by the German author Waldemar Bonsels, "The Adventures of Maya the Bee" relates the title character's flight from the hive in search of tastes more exotic than honey. The revival has new sets and props, but the same intricate rod and shadow puppets designed by Zofia Czechlewska. Adapted by Rachel Klein, with a score and lyrics by the jazz artist Nancy Harrow, who also conceived the show, "Maya" is a jazz musical for children, and how often do you encounter those? (The run is open-ended.) Saturdays at 10:30 a.m., 45 Bleecker Street, near Lafayette Street, East Village, (866) 811-4111, cultureproject.org; $25.

'Archaeology Zone: Discovering Treasures From Playgrounds to Palaces' (Friday, Sunday through Tuesday, and Thursday) Children will step into the shoes of an explorer like Indiana Jones in this permanent exhibition at the Jewish Museum, but the adventures will be purely scholarly. Still, there is plenty of excitement in analyzing artifacts like a jar handle, a clay jug and a bangle and figuring out the purpose behind ancient pieces like a Greek helmet and a bull-shaped vessel. This interactive show, for ages 3 to 10, also includes a recreated room from the Ottoman period (about 1900), where young archaeologists can dress in costume. From 11 a.m. to 5:45 p.m.; until 8 p.m. on Thursdays; 1109 Fifth Avenue, at 92nd Street, (212) 423-3200, thejewishmuseum.org. Free with admission: $12; $10 for 65+; $7.50 for students; free for under 12 and members.

A new Imax movie, this project chronicles their journey and the 40-year investigation of it by one impassioned scientist, Fred A. Urquhart. (Visitors can also see as many as 500 free-flying real butterflies in the special exhibition "The Butterfly Conservatory: Tropical Butterflies Alive in Winter," through May 27.) The film runs through July 7, hourly, from 10:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., American Museum of Natural History, Central Park West and 79th Street, (212) 769-5200, amnh.org.

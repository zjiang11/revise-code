During the first six weeks of the war in the Gaza Strip, Israel routinely dropped one of its biggest and most destructive bombs in areas it designated safe for civilians, according to an analysis of visual evidence by The New York Times. The regular use of 2,000-pound bombs suggests that even for Palestinians who followed every Israeli evacuation order and advisory, there was still no safety to be found in a war zone that is more dangerous for civilians than any in recent history.
Bombs of this size date to before the Vietnam War, but the United States military almost never drops them in densely populated areas anymore, munitions experts told the Times. These weapons unleash powerful blast waves and sharp metal fragments that can travel thousands of feet in every direction. Even smaller 500-pound bombs were routinely considered too big to use within cities during U.S. battles against the Islamic State group in Iraq and Syria, the experts said.
In response to questions about the use of 2,000-pound bombs, an Israeli military spokesperson said in a written statement to the Times that “in stark contrast to Hamas’ intentional attacks,” Israel “follows international law and takes feasible precautions to mitigate civilian harm.” The spokesperson also wrote that Israel’s priority was destroying Hamas and “questions of this kind will be looked into at a later stage.”
Days after the war began in October, Israel said it was going to keep civilians out of the crossfire by establishing safe areas and issuing evacuation orders. The Israeli military repeatedly ordered over 1 million people living in northern Gaza to leave their homes and move to south Gaza — the territory below the Wadi Gaza River Valley — for their safety.
But that area was far from safe.
The Times programmed an artificial intelligence tool to scan satellite imagery of south Gaza and detect potential bomb craters.
The AI tool flagged more than 1,600 possible craters. Each one was manually analyzed by Times journalists to weed out the false positives, such as circular shadows or bomb craters from a previous conflict. The remaining craters were measured to identify those that spanned roughly 40 feet across or more. Craters of this size in Gaza’s light, sandy soil generally only result from 2,000-pound bombs, munitions experts told the Times.
Ultimately, the Times identified 208 such craters in satellite imagery and drone footage, indicating that 2,000-pound bombs posed a pervasive threat to civilians across south Gaza.
The findings likely represent only a portion of the 2,000-pound bombs used in south Gaza during the first six weeks of the war because of the availability of aerial imagery and because, in some cases, the bomb may leave only small craters or no craters at all. Munition experts told the Times that the size of a 2,000-pound bomb’s crater can depend on the type of target and soil it hit, as well as how the bomb’s fuse was set.
Even satellite images of the biggest bomb craters do not convey the destruction unleashed on the ground.
One crater in central Khan Younis, for example, measuring at least 40 feet across, was left by an airstrike on a residential block on Oct. 26. In the aftermath, footage captured by witnesses and local journalists at the scene showed men frantically digging into mountains of mangled metal and pulverized concrete. Deep in the rubble, a seemingly lifeless arm suddenly appears, and a few minutes later, a face. It is a young boy, eyes wide open. He is alive.
Hours later, Israel claimed responsibility for the attack on social media, posting video of the strike and a photo of the alleged target: a top Hamas official who officials said was key in planning the Oct. 7 attack.
Israel has said Hamas is to blame for noncombatants injured and killed because it intentionally positions personnel and weapons in civilian areas and uses its own citizens as human shields.
But U.S. officials have said publicly that Israel must do more to protect civilians.
The Pentagon recently increased shipments to Israel of smaller bombs called GBU-39s, which U.S. officials told the Times can strike underground targets and are better suited to urban environments like Gaza.
The GBU-39 — one of the smallest bombs in Israel’s arsenal — creates a blast wave that can injure or kill people in open areas more than 1,000 feet away from the point of impact. Compare that to the MK-84, a 2,000-pound bomb commonly used by Israel: The typical danger zone stretches three times as far.
But the U.S. transfers of GBU-39s did not stop Israel from using 2,000-pound bombs in Gaza. U.S. officials told the Times that the Israeli military is heavily relying on them because Israel has many more 2,000-pound bombs in its arsenal, and they are more effective than the smaller bombs in destroying Hamas’ tunnel network as quickly as possible.
The officials also told the Times that in the war on Gaza, Israel has demonstrated a higher tolerance than the U.S. military for causing civilian casualties.
Nonetheless, the United States has not shown a willingness to cut off the flow of the weapons supplied to Israel. Just since October, the United States has sent more than 5,000 of the 2,000-pound bombs.
